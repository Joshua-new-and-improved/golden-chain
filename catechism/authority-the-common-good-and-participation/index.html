Authority, the common good and participation

What does the Catholic Church teach about authority, the common good and participation? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 1897–1927

In one sentence: Legitimate authority comes from God and serves the common good; every citizen is called to participate responsibly.

What the Catechism says

Every human community needs authority to ensure order and promote the common good. Its basis is in human nature and ultimately in God. Authority is exercised legitimately only when it seeks the common good by morally licit means; unjust laws do not bind in conscience.

The common good is the sum of the conditions of social life that allow people to reach their fulfilment more fully and easily. It includes respect for the person, social well-being and development, and peace (stability and security of a just order). Everyone should take part, according to their position and role, in promoting it: through personal responsibility at work and at home, and through public life.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 1897–1904 Authority: human society can be neither well ordered nor prosperous unless it has some people invested with legitimate authority to preserve its institutions and devote themselves to the common good. Authority is the quality by virtue of which persons or institutions make laws and give orders, and expect obedience. Every human community needs an authority; its foundation lies in human nature. "There is no authority except from God." The duty of obedience requires all to give due honour to authority. The choice of political regime and the appointment of rulers are left to the free decision of citizens. Authority does not derive its moral legitimacy from itself; it must act for the common good, employing morally licit means. If rulers enact unjust laws or take measures contrary to the moral order, such arrangements would not be binding in conscience. "A human law has the character of law to the extent that it accords with right reason… insofar as it falls short of right reason it is said to be an unjust law, and so has not so much the nature of law as of a kind of violence" (Aquinas). It is preferable that each power be balanced by other powers: the rule of law.

§ 1905–1912 The common good: "the sum total of social conditions which allow people, either as groups or as individuals, to reach their fulfilment more fully and more easily". It consists of three essential elements: respect for the person as such (fundamental and inalienable rights, freedom of conscience and religion); the social well-being and development of the group (making accessible what is needed to lead a truly human life: food, clothing, health, work, education and culture, information, the right to found a family); and peace, the stability and security of a just order. It is the purpose of the state to defend and promote the common good. Human interdependence is increasing: a universal common good concerns the whole human family.

§ 1913–1917 Responsibility and participation: participation is the voluntary and generous engagement of a person in social interchange. It is necessary that all participate, each according to his position and role, in promoting the common good. This obligation is inherent in the dignity of the human person. Participation is achieved first by taking charge of the areas for which one assumes personal responsibility: care for one's family, conscientious work. As far as possible citizens should take an active part in public life. As with any ethical obligation, participation requires conversion; fraud and other subterfuges by which some escape the constraints of law and civic obligation must be firmly condemned.

Words to know

Common good
The social conditions that allow everyone to flourish more fully and easily.

In the Bible

  • Rom 13:1-7: be subject to higher powers
  • Acts 5:29: we ought to obey God rather than men

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST I-II q.96 a.4: Unjust laws, contrary to the human good or to God, are more violence than law and do not bind in conscience.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “Human law has the nature of law insofar as it partakes of right reason… insofar as it deviates from reason it is called unjust law, and has the nature not of law but of violence.” (St Thomas Aquinas (ST I-II q.93 a.3 ad 2))
  • “Do not live entirely isolated, having retreated into yourselves… but gather instead to seek the common good together.” (Letter of Barnabas 4.10 (2nd century))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Christians must obey every law.
    What the Church teaches: Unjust laws contrary to the moral order are not binding in conscience; legitimate authority must serve the common good (§§ 1902–1903).
  • Not quite: The common good means the greatest good of the majority.
    What the Church teaches: It is the conditions allowing everyone to flourish, starting with respect for each person's rights (§§ 1906–1907).
