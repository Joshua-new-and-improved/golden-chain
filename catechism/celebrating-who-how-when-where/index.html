Celebrating: who, how, when, where

What does the Catholic Church teach about celebrating: who, how, when, where? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 1135–1209

In one sentence: The whole Christ, head and members, celebrates, through signs, words and music, in the rhythm of the Church's year, in churches that are houses of prayer.

What the Catechism says

Who celebrates? The whole Christ: the heavenly liturgy of angels and saints, and the assembly on earth, each with its own role. How? Through signs and symbols drawn from creation, human life and salvation history; through word and action; through singing, music and holy images.

When? Sunday, the Lord's Day, is the foundation of the liturgical year. Easter is the "feast of feasts". The year unfolds the whole mystery of Christ, and the Liturgy of the Hours sanctifies the day. Where? Worship is not tied to one place, since Christ is the true temple, but churches are signs of the Church on earth and houses of prayer. Different liturgical traditions (Latin, Byzantine and others) express the one mystery.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 1136–1144 Who celebrates? The liturgy is the action of the whole Christ, head and body. Those who celebrate in heaven: the heavenly powers, all creation, the servants of the Old and New Covenants, the new People of God, the martyrs, the Mother of God, the Bride of the Lamb. On earth it is the whole community, the Body of Christ united with its Head; the baptised offer themselves as spiritual sacrifices. Not all members have the same function: some are called by Holy Orders to act in the person of Christ the Head; there are also particular ministries (servers, readers, commentators, choir).

§ 1145–1162 How is it celebrated? Through signs and symbols drawn from human life (washing, anointing, breaking bread, sharing a cup), from creation (light, water, fire, oil, bread, wine), and from the Old Covenant (circumcision, anointing, the Passover). Christ himself used signs to heal and teach. Words and actions belong together: the Liturgy of the Word is an integral part of every sacrament. Singing and music are integral to solemn liturgy: "he who sings prays twice". Holy images (icons) of Christ, Mary, the angels and saints are justified by the Incarnation; contemplating them nourishes faith.

§ 1163–1178 When is it celebrated? The liturgical year unfolds the whole mystery of Christ. Sunday, the Lord's Day, "the first day" and "the eighth day", is the foundation and kernel of the whole liturgical year. Easter is the "feast of feasts", the "solemnity of solemnities". In the sanctoral cycle the Church venerates Mary and the saints. The Liturgy of the Hours, the public prayer of the Church, extends the Eucharist through the day; it is the prayer of the whole People of God.

§ 1179–1186 Where is it celebrated? Christian worship is not restricted to any one place: Christ is the true temple, and believers are living stones. But when religious freedom allows, Christians build churches: houses of prayer where the Eucharist is celebrated and reserved. Key places: the altar, the tabernacle, the chair, the ambo (lectern), the baptistery, and the place for the sacrament of Penance.

§ 1200–1209 Liturgical diversity and the unity of the mystery: from the first community of Jerusalem, the Church's liturgy has taken different forms in different places and cultures, expressing the one mystery of Christ. The liturgical traditions or rites in use today are the Latin (chiefly the Roman rite) and the Eastern rites: Byzantine, Alexandrian or Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Maronite and Chaldean. The Church holds all lawfully recognised rites to be of equal right and dignity. The criterion is unity in the apostolic faith; liturgical diversity can enrich the Church.

Words to know

Liturgical year
The yearly unfolding of Christ's mystery, from Advent to Christ the King.
Rite
A liturgical tradition, such as the Latin, Byzantine, Coptic or Syro-Malabar.

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Only the priest celebrates Mass; the people watch.
    What the Church teaches: The whole assembly, the Body united with its Head, celebrates, each according to its role (§§ 1140–1144).
  • Not quite: Icons and statues are idols.
    What the Church teaches: Holy images are justified by the Incarnation and point beyond themselves to Christ and the saints (§§ 1159–1162).
  • Not quite: Eastern Catholics are less Catholic than Latin Catholics.
    What the Church teaches: All lawfully recognised rites have equal right and dignity (§ 1203).
