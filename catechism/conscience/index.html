Conscience

What does the Catholic Church teach about conscience? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 1776–1802

In one sentence: Conscience is reason's judgement about the goodness of an act; it must be formed, followed, and corrected when it errs.

What the Catechism says

Deep within, we discover a law we did not give ourselves but must obey: conscience, our most secret core and sanctuary, where we are alone with God. Conscience is a judgement of reason by which a person recognises the moral quality of a concrete act, before, during and after.

Conscience must be informed and enlightened: formation is a lifelong task, guided by the Word of God, prayer, the advice of others, and the Church's teaching. We must always obey the certain judgement of our conscience. But conscience can err: ignorance can be culpable (when a person takes little trouble to find the truth) or not. Some rules always apply: never do evil that good may come of it; the Golden Rule; and respect for others' consciences.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 1776–1778 "Deep within his conscience man discovers a law which he has not laid upon himself but which he must obey. Its voice, ever calling him to love and to do what is good and to avoid evil, sounds in his heart… For man has in his heart a law inscribed by God… His conscience is man's most secret core and his sanctuary. There he is alone with God whose voice echoes in his depths" (Gaudium et Spes 16). Moral conscience, present at the heart of the person, enjoins him at the appropriate moment to do good and avoid evil. Conscience is a judgement of reason whereby the human person recognises the moral quality of a concrete act he is going to perform, is in the process of performing, or has already completed. Conscience is "a messenger of him, who, both in nature and in grace, speaks to us behind a veil" (Newman).

§ 1779–1782 It is important for every person to be sufficiently present to himself in order to hear and follow the voice of his conscience; this requirement of interiority is all the more necessary as life often distracts us from any reflection, self-examination or introspection. The dignity of the human person implies and requires uprightness of moral conscience. Man has the right to act in conscience and in freedom so as personally to make moral decisions. "He must not be forced to act contrary to his conscience. Nor must he be prevented from acting according to his conscience, especially in religious matters."

§ 1783–1785 The formation of conscience: conscience must be informed and moral judgement enlightened. A well-formed conscience is upright and truthful; it formulates its judgements according to reason, in conformity with the true good willed by the wisdom of the Creator. The education of conscience is a lifelong task. It guards against fear, selfishness and pride, resentment arising from guilt, and feelings of complacency. In the formation of conscience the Word of God is the light for our path; we must also examine our conscience before the Lord's Cross, assisted by the gifts of the Spirit, the witness or advice of others, and the authoritative teaching of the Church.

§ 1786–1789 To choose in accord with conscience: some rules apply in every case. One may never do evil so that good may result from it. The Golden Rule: "Whatever you wish that men would do to you, do so to them." Charity always proceeds by way of respect for one's neighbour and his conscience.

§ 1790–1794 Erroneous judgement: a human being must always obey the certain judgement of his conscience. If he were deliberately to act against it, he would condemn himself. Yet it can happen that conscience is in ignorance and makes erroneous judgements. This ignorance can often be imputed to personal responsibility: when a man "takes little trouble to find out what is true and good, or when conscience is by degrees almost blinded through the habit of committing sin". If the ignorance is invincible, or the judgement erroneous without the responsibility of the moral subject, the evil committed cannot be imputed to him; it nonetheless remains an evil, a privation, a disorder. One must therefore work to correct the errors of moral conscience. Sources of errors: ignorance of Christ and his Gospel, bad example, enslavement to one's passions, assertion of a mistaken notion of autonomy of conscience, rejection of the Church's authority and teaching, lack of conversion and charity.

Words to know

Conscience
A judgement of practical reason about the moral quality of a concrete act.
Erroneous conscience
A conscience that judges wrongly; culpable if one neglected to seek the truth.

In the Bible

  • Rom 2:14-16: the work of the law written in their hearts
  • 1 John 3:19-21: if our heart reprehend us not

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST I-II q.19 a.5: Acting against conscience is always wrong, even when conscience errs.
  • ST I-II q.19 a.6: Yet an erroneous conscience does not always excuse: not if the ignorance is voluntary or negligent.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “Conscience is the aboriginal Vicar of Christ.” (St John Henry Newman (Letter to the Duke of Norfolk 5))
  • “Conscience is not a power but an act: the application of knowledge to what we do.” (St Thomas Aquinas (ST I q.79 a.13))
  • “I will not, for the whole world, set my conscience aside; in this matter I must look to my own soul.” (St Thomas More (letter to his daughter Margaret from the Tower, 1534, paraphrased))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Conscience means my feelings or personal opinion.
    What the Church teaches: Conscience is a judgement of reason about the moral quality of a concrete act, which must be formed by truth (§§ 1778, 1783).
  • Not quite: If I follow my conscience I am always right.
    What the Church teaches: One must follow a certain conscience, but it can err; we have a duty to form it, and culpable ignorance does not excuse (§§ 1790–1791).
  • Not quite: Forming my conscience by Church teaching means not thinking for myself.
    What the Church teaches: The Church's teaching is one of the lights by which reason judges well, alongside Scripture, prayer, advice and the gifts of the Spirit (§ 1785).

Psychology connection

Moral psychology shows that intuitions can be quick and biased, with reasons supplied afterwards (Haidt, 2001). The Church's insistence on forming conscience, slowly, with others and with the truth, is a safeguard against exactly this.

Scrupulosity, religious or moral OCD, is a disorder where conscience-like anxiety becomes compulsive: repeated confessing, reassurance-seeking, intrusive fears of sin. The Catechism's picture of a well-formed conscience (upright, truthful, guarding against "resentment arising from guilt") is the healthy contrast. Exposure and response prevention (ERP) is the evidence-based treatment, often best done with a therapist and a confessor working together (Abramowitz & Jacoby 2014).

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand conscience as a judgement of reason, the duty to form it, and the difference between invincible and culpable ignorance.

  1. How would you explain to a friend the difference between conscience and feelings?
  2. What forms (or deforms) consciences today: media, friends, habits?
  3. What is the difference between invincible and culpable (vincible) ignorance?

Activity: Introduce the daily examen: five minutes at night to give thanks, review the day, notice where you followed or ignored conscience, ask forgiveness, and resolve. Practise it together.
