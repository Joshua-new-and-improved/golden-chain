Freedom

What does the Catholic Church teach about freedom? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 1730–1748

In one sentence: Freedom is the power to act deliberately; it reaches perfection when directed to God, and the more we do good, the freer we become.

What the Catechism says

God created us rational, giving us dignity as persons who can initiate and control our own actions. Freedom is the power, rooted in reason and will, to act or not to act, and so to shape one's own life. It makes us responsible for our acts to the extent that they are voluntary.

Freedom is not the right to say or do anything. The more one does good, the freer one becomes; choosing evil is an abuse of freedom that leads to slavery to sin. Responsibility can be diminished or even removed by ignorance, inadvertence, duress, fear, habits, inordinate attachments and other psychological or social factors. Christ's grace does not compete with our freedom: it sets it free.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 1730–1732 God created man a rational being, conferring on him the dignity of a person who can initiate and control his own actions. "God willed that man should be left in the hand of his own counsel" (Sirach 15:14). Freedom is the power, rooted in reason and will, to act or not to act, to do this or that, and so to perform deliberate actions on one's own responsibility. Freedom is a force for growth and maturity in truth and goodness; it attains its perfection when directed toward God, our beatitude. As long as freedom has not bound itself definitively to its ultimate good, there is the possibility of choosing between good and evil.

§ 1733–1738 The more one does what is good, the freer one becomes. There is no true freedom except in the service of what is good and just; choosing to disobey and do evil is an abuse of freedom and leads to "the slavery of sin". Freedom makes man responsible for his acts to the extent that they are voluntary. Imputability and responsibility for an action can be diminished or even nullified by ignorance, inadvertence, duress, fear, habit, inordinate attachments, and other psychological or social factors. Every human person has the natural right to be recognised as a free and responsible being; the right to the exercise of freedom, especially in moral and religious matters, must be recognised and protected by civil authority, within the limits of the common good and public order.

§ 1739–1742 Freedom and sin: man's freedom is limited and fallible. By refusing God's plan of love, he deceived himself and became a slave to sin. The exercise of freedom does not imply a right to say or do everything; it is false to maintain that man, "the subject of this freedom", is "an individual who is fully self-sufficient and whose finality is the satisfaction of his own interests in the enjoyment of earthly goods". Freedom and grace: by his glorious Cross Christ has won salvation for all men. The grace of Christ is not in the slightest way a rival of our freedom when this freedom accords with the sense of the true and the good that God has put in the human heart. The more docile we are to the promptings of grace, the more we grow in inner freedom.

Words to know

Imputability
The degree to which an act can be attributed to someone as its responsible author.

In the Bible

  • Gal 5:1, 13: called unto liberty
  • John 8:31-36: the truth shall make you free

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST I-II q.6 a.8: Ignorance can make an act involuntary, depending on whether it precedes, accompanies or follows the will.
  • ST I q.83 a.1: Man has free will because he acts by judgement of reason, not by instinct alone.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “Man is rational and therefore like God; he is created with free will and is master over his acts.” (St Irenaeus (Against Heresies IV.4.3))
  • “The will is truly free when it is not the slave of vices and sins.” (St Augustine (City of God XIV.11))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Freedom means doing whatever I want.
    What the Church teaches: Freedom is ordered to the good: the more one does good, the freer one becomes; evil enslaves (§ 1733).
  • Not quite: Grace competes with freedom: more God means less me.
    What the Church teaches: Grace is not a rival of freedom; docility to grace increases inner freedom (§ 1742).
  • Not quite: Any factor like fear or habit means I'm never responsible.
    What the Church teaches: Such factors can diminish or even remove imputability, but freedom remains real and we are responsible to the extent acts are voluntary (§§ 1734–1735).

Psychology connection

CCC 1735 is one of the clearest bridges to clinical psychology: compulsive behaviours, addiction, trauma responses and severe anxiety can genuinely reduce freedom, and so culpability, without making the acts good. That's why moral judgement of acts and pastoral judgement of persons are distinct.

§ 1735's list (ignorance, duress, fear, habit, psychological factors) parallels how forensic and clinical psychology assess capacity and diminished responsibility. Self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci 2000) likewise distinguishes autonomous from controlled motivation: being driven by compulsion is not the same as acting freely.

To think about

Is there a habit that makes you less free? What small step would increase your freedom?

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand freedom as power for the good, the factors that diminish responsibility, and the harmony of grace and freedom.

  1. Can you think of a habit that has made you less free? One that has made you more free?
  2. How does § 1735 help us judge others (and ourselves) with mercy without denying responsibility?
  3. What is the difference between "freedom from" and "freedom for"?

Activity: Make two columns: "things that enslave" and "things that set free". Discuss how a virtue (like temperance) is a kind of freedom.
