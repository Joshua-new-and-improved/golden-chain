Grace and justification

What does the Catholic Church teach about grace and justification? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 1987–2029

In one sentence: God justifies us by grace, freely, through faith and Baptism; our merit is itself his gift, and all are called to holiness.

What the Catechism says

The grace of the Holy Spirit can justify us: cleanse us from sin and communicate God's righteousness through faith and Baptism. Justification is the most excellent work of God's love; it is God's free initiative, yet it involves our free cooperation.

Grace is favour, the free and undeserved help God gives us to respond to his call. Sanctifying grace is a stable gift that heals and divinises us; actual graces are God's interventions at particular moments. Merit: in strict justice we can merit nothing from God, but by his loving plan, our good works done in grace merit reward, because it is God who crowns his own gifts in us. All Christians are called to holiness: the fullness of Christian life and the perfection of charity.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 1987–1995 Justification: the grace of the Holy Spirit has the power to justify us, that is, to cleanse us from our sins and to communicate to us "the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ" and through Baptism. Justification is first the work of conversion: man turns towards God and away from sin, and so accepts forgiveness and righteousness from on high. "Justification is not only the remission of sins, but also the sanctification and renewal of the interior man" (Trent). It detaches man from sin, and purifies his heart. It establishes cooperation between God's grace and man's freedom: man assents to the Word of God, and grace prevents (goes before), prepares and elicits this free response. Justification has been merited for us by the Passion of Christ. It is conferred in Baptism. It is "the most excellent work of God's love" (Augustine), greater even than the creation of heaven and earth.

§ 1996–2005 Grace: our justification comes from the grace of God. Grace is favour, the free and undeserved help that God gives us to respond to his call to become children of God, partakers of the divine nature and of eternal life. It is a participation in the life of God. The preparation of man for the reception of grace is already a work of grace. Sanctifying grace (habitual grace) is the gratuitous gift of his life that God makes to us, infused by the Spirit into our soul to heal it of sin and sanctify it: a stable and supernatural disposition. Actual graces are God's interventions, whether at the beginning of conversion or in the course of the work of sanctification. There are also sacramental graces, special graces (charisms) and graces of state. Grace, being supernatural, escapes our experience and cannot be known except by faith; we cannot rely on feelings to deduce that we are justified. But reflection on God's blessings in our life is a guarantee that grace is at work in us. St Joan of Arc, asked if she was in God's grace: "If I am not, may it please God to put me in it; if I am, may it please God to keep me there."

§ 2006–2011 Merit: with regard to God, there is no strict right to any merit on the part of man; between God and us there is an immeasurable inequality. The merit of man before God in the Christian life arises from the fact that God has freely chosen to associate man with the work of his grace. The fatherly action of God is first on his own initiative, and then follows man's free acting through his collaboration. Since the initiative belongs to God in the order of grace, no one can merit the initial grace of forgiveness and justification. Moved by the Spirit and charity, we can then merit for ourselves and others the graces needed for sanctification and for eternal life. The charity of Christ is the source in us of all our merits. "Our merits are God's gifts" (Augustine). "In the evening of this life, I shall appear before you with empty hands" (Thérèse of Lisieux).

§ 2012–2016 Christian holiness: "we know that in everything God works for good with those who love him". All Christians in any state or walk of life are called to the fullness of Christian life and to the perfection of charity. Spiritual progress tends toward ever more intimate union with Christ, sometimes called "mystical" union. The way of perfection passes by way of the Cross: there is no holiness without renunciation and spiritual battle. The children of our holy mother the Church rightly hope for the grace of final perseverance.

Words to know

Justification
Being made righteous: forgiveness of sins and inner renewal, by grace.
Sanctifying grace
The habitual gift of God's own life in the soul.

In the Bible

  • Rom 3:21-26: justified freely by his grace
  • Eph 2:8-10: by grace you are saved
  • Phil 2:12-13: it is God who worketh in you

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST I-II q.113 a.9: The justification of the ungodly is a greater work than the creation of heaven and earth.
  • ST I-II q.114 a.1: We merit only by God's ordination: he crowns his own gifts.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “God created us without us, but he did not will to save us without us.” (St Augustine (Sermon 169.11.13))
  • “In the evening of this life, I shall appear before you with empty hands, for I do not ask you, Lord, to count my works.” (St Thérèse of Lisieux (Act of Offering to Merciful Love))
  • “If I am not, may it please God to put me in it; if I am, may it please God to keep me there.” (St Joan of Arc (at her trial))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Catholics believe we earn salvation by good works.
    What the Church teaches: No one can merit the initial grace of justification; it is God's free gift. Our merits are themselves God's gifts (§§ 2007–2011).
  • Not quite: Justification is only a legal declaration; nothing changes inside us.
    What the Church teaches: Justification is remission of sins and also interior sanctification and renewal (§ 1989).
  • Not quite: If I feel close to God I'm in grace; if not, I'm not.
    What the Church teaches: Grace escapes our experience and cannot be deduced from feelings (§ 2005).

To think about

Thank God today for one grace you know you did not deserve.

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand justification, grace (sanctifying and actual) and merit in Catholic teaching, and the universal call to holiness. This section is especially useful in conversations with evangelical Christians.

  1. How would you explain to an evangelical friend that Catholics believe salvation is by grace?
  2. What is the difference between sanctifying and actual grace?
  3. What do Thérèse's "empty hands" teach about merit?

Activity: Read the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification (Catholic-Lutheran, 1999), § 15: "By grace alone, in faith in Christ's saving work and not because of any merit on our part, we are accepted by God." Discuss what Catholics and Lutherans agreed and where they still differ.
