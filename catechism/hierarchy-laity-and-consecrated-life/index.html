Hierarchy, laity and consecrated life

What does the Catholic Church teach about hierarchy, laity and consecrated life? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 871–945

In one sentence: All the baptised share Christ's mission: the ordained serve as shepherds, the laity sanctify the world, and the consecrated witness to the Kingdom.

What the Catechism says

Among all the faithful there is a true equality of dignity, and a diversity of ministries. Christ instituted the hierarchy to serve: the college of bishops with the Pope as head, who teach, sanctify and govern, with priests and deacons as their co-workers. The Pope is the perpetual and visible source of unity.

The lay faithful have the vocation to seek the Kingdom by engaging in temporal affairs and ordering them to God: in families, work, politics and culture. Consecrated life (religious, hermits, consecrated virgins, secular institutes) professes the evangelical counsels of poverty, chastity and obedience as a sign of the world to come.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 871–873 "The Christian faithful are those who, inasmuch as they have been incorporated into Christ through Baptism, have been constituted as the people of God." Among them there is a true equality of dignity and action, and a diversity of ministry. Clergy (the ordained) and laity both come from among the faithful, and some of both are called to consecrated life.

§ 874–896 Christ instituted the ministries in his Church for the good of the whole Body. The ordained receive a mission and power to act in the person of Christ the Head; this ministry is service. It has a collegial character (the apostles were a college with Peter as head) and a personal one (each is called by name). The Pope, Bishop of Rome and Peter's successor, is "the perpetual and visible source and foundation of the unity both of the bishops and of the whole company of the faithful", with full, supreme and universal power. The college of bishops has supreme authority only together with the Pope. Each bishop is the visible principle of unity in his particular Church. The offices of teaching (with the charism of infallibility in defining faith and morals), sanctifying and governing.

§ 897–913 The laity are all the faithful except those in Holy Orders and the religious state. Their vocation is to seek the Kingdom of God by engaging in temporal affairs and directing them according to God's will: initiative in discovering ways for Christian truth to permeate social, political and economic realities. They share in Christ's priestly office (offering their work, prayer, family life, even hardships), prophetic office (evangelising by word and life), and kingly office (overcoming sin in themselves, making the world's structures more just). They can cooperate in the exercise of the Church's power of governance and in many ministries.

§ 914–933 Consecrated life, characterised by the public profession of the evangelical counsels of poverty, chastity and obedience in a stable state of life recognised by the Church, is a gift to the Church. It takes many forms: hermits, consecrated virgins and widows, religious life (in monasteries and active congregations), secular institutes, and societies of apostolic life. Its deepest meaning is a more intimate consecration to God, a sign of the world to come, and service of the Church's mission.

Words to know

Infallibility
The charism by which the Pope, and bishops in union with him, cannot err when defining doctrine of faith or morals.
Evangelical counsels
Poverty, chastity and obedience, freely vowed.

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: The laity are second-class Catholics who just "help Father".
    What the Church teaches: All the baptised share equal dignity; the laity have their own vocation to sanctify the world (§§ 872, 898).
  • Not quite: Papal infallibility means the Pope never makes mistakes.
    What the Church teaches: It is a limited charism: when the Pope, as supreme teacher, defines a doctrine of faith or morals; it doesn't cover personal opinions or sinlessness (§ 891).
