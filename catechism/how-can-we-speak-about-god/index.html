How can we speak about God?

How can we speak about God? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 39–43

In one sentence: Our words about God are true, but God is always greater than anything they can say.

What the Catechism says

Because we can know God, we can speak about him, to everyone, including people of other religions and none. But our words come from creatures, so they always fall short.

Every creature reflects something of God, above all the human person made in his image. So we name God from the perfections we see (goodness, truth, beauty) while purifying our words of everything limited, imperfect or merely human. Between Creator and creature, every likeness hides a greater unlikeness (Fourth Lateran Council). Our language is truly about God, but never captures him.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 39 In defending reason's ability to know God, the Church expresses her confidence in the possibility of speaking about him to all people: to other religions, to philosophy and science, and to unbelievers and atheists.

§ 40 Since our knowledge of God is limited, our language about him is equally so. We can name God only by taking creatures as our starting point, in accordance with our limited human ways of knowing and thinking.

§ 41 All creatures bear a certain resemblance to God, especially the human person made in his image. Their manifold perfections (truth, goodness, beauty) reflect God's infinite perfection, so we can name God from them, "for from the greatness and beauty of created things comes a corresponding perception of their Creator" (Wis 13:5).

§ 42 God transcends all creatures. We must therefore continually purify our language of everything limited, image-bound or imperfect, so as not to confuse God, "the inexpressible, the incomprehensible, the invisible, the ungraspable", with our human representations. Our words always fall short.

§ 43 Yet in speaking about God like this, our language really does attain God himself, though it cannot express him in his infinite simplicity. Between Creator and creature no similitude can be expressed without implying an even greater dissimilitude (Lateran IV); and "concerning God, we cannot grasp what he is, but only what he is not" (St Thomas).

Words to know

Analogy
A word used of God and creatures in a related but not identical sense: God is "good" truly, but infinitely more than any creature.
Apophatic
Speaking of God by saying what he is not (infinite, immortal, invisible).

In the Bible

  • Isa 55:8-9: my thoughts are not your thoughts
  • Wis 13:5: by the greatness of creatures

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST I q.13 a.5: Names like "good" and "wise" are said of God neither in exactly the same sense (univocally) nor in an unrelated one (equivocally), but by analogy.
  • SCG I.30: "Concerning God we cannot grasp what he is, but only what he is not": the line the Catechism quotes (CCC 43).

From the Fathers and saints

  • “Concerning God, we cannot grasp what he is, but only what he is not, and how other things stand in relation to him.” (St Thomas Aquinas (SCG I.30))
  • “If you have understood, then what you have understood is not God.” (St Augustine (Sermon 117))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Since God is beyond words, nothing we say about him is true.
    What the Church teaches: Our language really attains God, though it never captures him fully (§ 43).
  • Not quite: Calling God "Father" means God is male.
    What the Church teaches: Such words come from creatures and must be purified of their limits; God is neither man nor woman (§§ 42, 239).

To think about

What image or word for God helps you most? What does it fail to capture?

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand analogy: our words about God are true but always fall short.

  1. What does "good" mean when we say a meal is good, a person is good, and God is good?
  2. Why can't any image of God be complete?
  3. How could this help when talking with people of other religions (§ 39)?

Activity: Each person writes one word for God and then two ways it is true of God, and one way God is infinitely more than that word.
