Marriage: fidelity and fruitfulness

What does the Catholic Church teach about marriage: fidelity and fruitfulness? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 2360–2400

In one sentence: The love of husband and wife is faithful and fruitful; adultery, divorce, contraception and cohabitation offend against it.

What the Catechism says

Conjugal love has two ends: the good of the spouses and the transmission of life. These two meanings, unitive and procreative, cannot be separated. Spouses are called to responsible parenthood; periodic continence (natural family planning) respects this, whereas contraception separates the two meanings and is wrong. Children are a gift, not a right; techniques that dissociate the sexual act from procreation are morally unacceptable.

Offences against the dignity of marriage include adultery, divorce (which claims to break a valid contract), polygamy, incest, so-called free unions and trial marriages. The separated and the divorced who have not remarried can be exemplary witnesses; the divorced and civilly remarried remain members of the Church and should be accompanied with care.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 2360–2365 Sexuality is ordered to the conjugal love of man and woman. In marriage the physical intimacy of the spouses becomes a sign and pledge of spiritual communion. "Sexuality… is realised in a truly human way only if it is an integral part of the love by which a man and woman commit themselves totally to one another until death." Tobias and Sarah's prayer expresses this. The spouses' union achieves the twofold end of marriage: the good of the spouses themselves and the transmission of life. These two meanings or values cannot be separated without altering the couple's spiritual life and compromising the goods of marriage and the future of the family. Fidelity expresses constancy in keeping one's given word; God is faithful.

§ 2366–2372 The fecundity of marriage: fecundity is a gift, an end of marriage, for conjugal love naturally tends to be fruitful. A child does not come from outside as something added on to mutual love; he springs from the very heart of that mutual giving. "Each and every marriage act must remain open to the transmission of life" (Humanae Vitae 11). Responsible parenthood: for just reasons, spouses may wish to space the births of their children; it is their duty to make certain that their desire is not motivated by selfishness but is in conformity with the generosity appropriate to responsible parenthood. Periodic continence, methods of birth regulation based on self-observation and the use of infertile periods (natural family planning), is in conformity with the objective criteria of morality. "Every action which, whether in anticipation of the conjugal act, or in its accomplishment, or in the development of its natural consequences, proposes, whether as an end or as a means, to render procreation impossible" is intrinsically evil. The state may legitimately inform and take measures to regulate population, but not by authoritarian or coercive means.

§ 2373–2379 The gift of a child: Scripture and the Church's practice see in large families a sign of God's blessing and the parents' generosity. Couples who discover they are sterile suffer greatly. Research aimed at reducing human sterility is to be encouraged on condition it is placed at the service of the human person. Techniques that entail the dissociation of husband and wife by the intrusion of a third person (donor sperm or egg, surrogate uterus) are gravely immoral. Techniques involving only the married couple (homologous IVF) are perhaps less reprehensible, yet remain morally unacceptable, because they dissociate the sexual act from the procreative act and entrust life and identity to the power of doctors and biologists. A child is not something owed to one, but is a gift. Couples who cannot have children can unite themselves with the Lord's Cross, give expression to their generosity by adopting abandoned children, or perform demanding services for others.

§ 2380–2391 Offences against the dignity of marriage: adultery is marital infidelity; it is an injustice and a grave sin. Divorce is a grave offence against the natural law: it claims to break the contract to which the spouses freely consented to live with each other till death. The separation of spouses while maintaining the marriage bond can be legitimate in certain cases provided for by canon law; if civil divorce remains the only possible way of ensuring certain legal rights, the care of children or the protection of inheritance, it can be tolerated and does not constitute a moral offence. It can happen that one spouse is the innocent victim of a divorce decreed by civil law; this spouse has not contravened the moral law. Polygamy is not in accord with the moral law; a polygamist who becomes Christian has a grave duty in justice to honour obligations contracted toward former wives and children. Incest, and sexual abuse perpetrated by adults on children or adolescents entrusted to their care, are gravely evil. Free union and "trial marriage" are contrary to the dignity of marriage.

Words to know

Unitive and procreative
The two inseparable meanings of the marital act.
Responsible parenthood
Generous and prudent decisions about having children, respecting the moral law.

In the Bible

  • Gen 1:28: increase and multiply
  • Heb 13:4: marriage honourable in all
  • Mal 2:14-16: let none despise the wife of his youth

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • SCG III.122: Thomas argues that sexual acts are rightly ordered when they respect their natural end and the good of the children who may come from them.
  • ST Supplement q.49 a.2: The three goods of marriage (Augustine): offspring, fidelity and the sacrament.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “Grant that she and I may find mercy, and that we may grow old together.” (Tobit 8:7)
  • “This love is fully human… total… faithful and exclusive until death… and fecund.” (St Paul VI (Humanae Vitae 9, 1968))
  • “When we had our children, our ideas changed: we lived only for them; it was our happiness.” (Sts Louis and Zélie Martin (letter of St Zélie, paraphrased))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: The Church wants couples to have as many children as possible.
    What the Church teaches: Spouses may, for just reasons, responsibly space births, using natural methods; generosity, not maximum numbers, is asked (§§ 2368–2370).
  • Not quite: Natural family planning is just Catholic contraception.
    What the Church teaches: NFP works with the body's natural cycle and keeps each act open to life; contraception renders procreation impossible (§ 2370).
  • Not quite: A divorced Catholic is automatically excommunicated.
    What the Church teaches: Divorce itself does not excommunicate; an innocent spouse may not have sinned at all; the difficulty arises with remarriage without annulment (§§ 2383–2386, 1650).
  • Not quite: Infertile couples are second-class.
    What the Church teaches: The Church encourages research to heal infertility and honours their many forms of fruitfulness, including adoption (§§ 2375, 2379).

To think about

What questions do you have about the Church's teaching on marriage? Bring them to a priest or a trusted mentor.

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand the unitive and procreative meanings of marriage, responsible parenthood and NFP, the gift of a child, and offences against marriage, with sensitivity to those who are divorced or infertile.

  1. Why does the Church hold that the unitive and procreative meanings must not be separated?
  2. What does "responsible parenthood" mean in practice for a young South African couple?
  3. How can a parish support couples facing infertility, or families affected by divorce?

Activity: Invite a married couple who teach natural family planning (for example the Billings or Creighton method, or a local Catholic family-life office) to explain how it works and why they chose it.
