Penance and Reconciliation

What does the Catholic Church teach about penance and Reconciliation? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 1420–1470

In one sentence: Christ gave the Church a sacrament to forgive sins after Baptism: contrition, confession and satisfaction, with the priest's absolution.

What the Catechism says

The new life of Baptism does not remove our weakness, so Christ instituted this sacrament for the baptised who sin. It has many names: conversion, Penance, Confession, forgiveness, Reconciliation. It answers Christ's call to conversion, which continues all through Christian life.

Interior penance (a radical reorientation of life) comes first; it shows itself in fasting, prayer and almsgiving. The sacrament has the acts of the penitent (contrition, confession of all mortal sins not yet confessed, and satisfaction) and God's action through the priest's absolution. Confession of venial sins is strongly recommended. The priest is bound by the absolute seal of confession. Its effects: reconciliation with God and with the Church, remission of eternal punishment, peace of conscience and spiritual strength.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 1420–1421 Through the sacraments of initiation we receive new life in Christ, but we carry it "in earthen vessels". This new life can be weakened and even lost by sin. The Lord Jesus, physician of our souls and bodies, willed that his Church continue his work of healing: this is the purpose of the two sacraments of healing, Penance and the Anointing of the Sick.

§ 1422–1426 It is called the sacrament of conversion (it makes sacramentally present Jesus' call to conversion), of Penance (a process of conversion, repentance and satisfaction), of confession (the disclosure of sins is an essential element, and also a "confession" of God's holiness and mercy), of forgiveness (God grants pardon and peace through the priest's absolution), and of Reconciliation (it imparts God's reconciling love). Why after Baptism? Baptismal grace does not abolish the frailty of human nature or the inclination to sin.

§ 1427–1433 The conversion of the baptised: Christ's call to conversion continues in Christians' lives: an uninterrupted task for the whole Church, which is "at once holy and always in need of purification". It is the movement of a "contrite heart" drawn by grace (Peter's conversion after his denial). Interior penance is a radical reorientation of our whole life, a return to God with all our heart, an end of sin, a turning away from evil, with repugnance towards the evil actions we have committed.

§ 1434–1439 Many forms of penance: Scripture and the Fathers insist above all on three: fasting, prayer and almsgiving, expressing conversion in relation to oneself, to God and to others. Also: reconciliation with one's neighbour, tears of repentance, concern for the salvation of others, the intercession of the saints, the practice of charity, revision of life, examination of conscience, spiritual direction, acceptance of suffering, enduring persecution for righteousness' sake. The seasons and days of penance (Lent, each Friday) are especially intense moments. The process of conversion is described in the parable of the prodigal son.

§ 1440–1449 The sacrament: sin is first an offence against God and a rupture of communion with him, and damages communion with the Church. Only God forgives sin; Jesus gave this power to the apostles: "Receive the Holy Spirit; whose sins you forgive are forgiven." Reconciliation with the Church is inseparable from reconciliation with God. Over the centuries the form changed: in the early Church, public penance for grave sins, sometimes once in a lifetime; from the 7th century, Irish missionaries spread private penance, opening the way to regular celebration. Through the changes the same fundamental structure remains: the acts of the penitent and the action of God through the bishop or priest.

§ 1450–1460 The acts of the penitent: contrition, confession and satisfaction. Contrition is "sorrow of the soul and detestation for the sin committed, together with the resolution not to sin again". Perfect contrition arises from love of God above all; it remits venial sins, and also mortal sins if it includes the firm resolution to confess as soon as possible. Imperfect contrition (attrition) arises from the ugliness of sin or fear of punishment; it is enough for the sacrament. Preparation by an examination of conscience in the light of God's word. Confession of sins: all mortal sins of which one is conscious after diligent examination must be confessed, even the most secret. Confession of venial sins (everyday faults) is strongly recommended. Satisfaction (penance): absolution takes away sin but does not remedy all the disorders sin has caused; the penitent must do something more to make amends, "do penance", which helps configure us to Christ.

§ 1461–1467 The minister: bishops and priests, by virtue of Holy Orders, have the power to forgive sins, exercising it with the faculty granted by the Church. Certain particularly grave sins incur excommunication; absolution from them is reserved, but any priest can absolve in danger of death. The confessor is a sign and instrument of God's merciful love, not master of God's forgiveness; he must have knowledge, prudence, respect and discretion. Every priest who hears confessions is bound under very severe penalties to keep absolute secrecy: the "sacramental seal", which admits of no exceptions.

§ 1468–1470 Effects: reconciliation with God, by which the penitent recovers sanctifying grace; reconciliation with the Church; remission of the eternal punishment incurred by mortal sins; remission, at least in part, of temporal punishments; peace and serenity of conscience, and spiritual consolation; and an increase of spiritual strength for the Christian battle. In this sacrament the sinner, placing himself before God's merciful judgement, anticipates in a certain way the judgement at the end of his earthly life.

Words to know

Contrition
Sorrow of soul and detestation for sin, with resolve not to sin again. "Perfect" if from love of God; "imperfect" (attrition) if from fear, still enough for the sacrament.
Seal of confession
The absolute secrecy binding every confessor.

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Why confess to a priest? I can confess directly to God.
    What the Church teaches: Christ gave the apostles the power to forgive; sin also wounds the Church, and the sacrament reconciles us with both (§§ 1441–1445).
  • Not quite: Imperfect contrition (fear of hell) isn't good enough.
    What the Church teaches: Attrition is enough for forgiveness in the sacrament (§ 1453).
  • Not quite: A priest could reveal my sins in some circumstances.
    What the Church teaches: The sacramental seal admits of no exceptions (§ 1467).

Psychology connection

Contrition is not shame or self-loathing, which psychology links with avoidance and lower wellbeing. It is sorrow for a wrong done, together with hope; research separates the two in a similar way (guilt tied to an action tends to motivate repair, shame about the self tends to motivate hiding: Tangney & Dearing, 2002).

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand conversion, the history and structure of Penance, the acts of the penitent, the seal and the effects, and feel confident about going to Confession.

  1. Why does Jesus' call to conversion apply to people who are already Christian?
  2. What is the difference between perfect and imperfect contrition?
  3. What is the most common thing that keeps people away from Confession, and how would you answer it?

Activity: Read Luke 15:11–32 (the prodigal son) and map it onto the acts of the penitent: coming to himself (examination), returning and confessing, the father's embrace (absolution), the feast (reconciliation). Then walk through how to make a confession.
