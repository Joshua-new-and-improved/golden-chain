Sacramentals and Christian funerals

What does the Catholic Church teach about sacramentals and Christian funerals? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 1667–1690

In one sentence: Sacramentals (blessings, holy water, rosaries) prepare us for grace; Christian funerals commend the dead to God.

What the Catechism says

Sacramentals are sacred signs instituted by the Church, which resemble the sacraments and dispose us to receive grace and to sanctify everyday life. Blessings come first: of persons, meals, objects and places. Some consecrate permanently (as with a church or a chalice). Exorcism is also a sacramental. Popular piety (relics, pilgrimages, the Rosary, medals) is to be encouraged, guided by the liturgy.

For the Christian, death is the last Passover. The funeral rite commends the dead to God's mercy, supports the community in hope, and expresses communion with the departed. It includes the greeting of the community, the Word, the Eucharist, and the farewell.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 1667–1670 Holy Mother Church has instituted sacramentals: sacred signs which bear a resemblance to the sacraments; they signify effects, particularly of a spiritual nature, obtained through the Church's intercession. By them people are disposed to receive the chief effect of the sacraments, and various occasions in life are rendered holy. They derive from the baptismal priesthood: every baptised person is called to be a "blessing" and to bless. They do not confer the grace of the Spirit in the way the sacraments do, but by the Church's prayer they prepare us to receive grace and dispose us to cooperate with it.

§ 1671–1673 Among sacramentals, blessings (of persons, meals, objects and places) come first. Every blessing praises God and prays for his gifts. Some blessings have a lasting importance because they consecrate persons to God (abbots, consecrated virgins, readers, acolytes, catechists) or reserve objects and places for liturgical use (churches, altars, holy oils, vessels and vestments, bells). Exorcism: when the Church asks publicly and authoritatively in Jesus' name that a person or object be protected against the power of the Evil One. In a simple form it is performed at Baptism; a solemn (major) exorcism can be performed only by a priest with the bishop's permission, after ascertaining that it is truly a question of the presence of the Evil One and not of an illness, especially psychological illness, which is the domain of medical science.

§ 1674–1676 Popular piety: besides the liturgy, Christian life is nourished by forms of piety rooted in different cultures: the veneration of relics, visits to sanctuaries, pilgrimages, processions, the Stations of the Cross, religious dances, the Rosary, medals. These extend the Church's liturgical life but do not replace it; they should harmonise with the liturgical seasons and lead to it. Pastoral discernment is needed to support and purify them.

§ 1680–1683 Christian funerals: all the sacraments, especially those of initiation, have as their goal the last Passover of the child of God, which leads him into the life of the Kingdom. For the Christian, the day of death inaugurates, at the end of sacramental life, the fulfilment of his new birth begun at Baptism. The Church, who as mother has borne the Christian sacramentally in her womb during his earthly pilgrimage, accompanies him at his journey's end, to surrender him "into the Father's hands".

§ 1684–1690 The funeral liturgy expresses communion with the deceased, the community's participation, and the proclamation of eternal life. It has four principal elements: the greeting of the community (a word of consolation); the Liturgy of the Word; the Eucharistic sacrifice, in which the Church expresses her efficacious communion with the departed; and the farewell (a dieu), commending the deceased to God.

Words to know

In the Bible

  • 1 Thess 4:12-17 (13-18): that you be not sorrowful

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST III q.65 a.1 ad 6: Thomas distinguishes the sacraments from other sacred signs and actions that prepare for them.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “From long experience I have learned that there is nothing like holy water to put devils to flight.” (St Teresa of Avila (Life 31.4))
  • “Lay this body anywhere; let not the care of it trouble you. This only I ask of you, that you remember me at the altar of the Lord, wherever you may be.” (St Monica (to Augustine, Confessions IX.11))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Holy water, medals and rosaries are lucky charms.
    What the Church teaches: Sacramentals work through the Church's prayer and our faith, preparing us for grace; treating them as magic is superstition (§§ 1670, 2111).
  • Not quite: Anything strange is a sign of the devil, so call an exorcist.
    What the Church teaches: The Church first ascertains that it is not an illness, especially psychological illness, which belongs to medicine (§ 1673).

Psychology connection

§ 1673 is an important point of contact with psychology: distress that looks "spiritual" may be psychosis, dissociation, severe anxiety or depression, and needs proper clinical assessment first.

To think about

Bless yourself with holy water next time you enter a church, recalling your Baptism.
