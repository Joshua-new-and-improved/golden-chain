Sin

What does the Catholic Church teach about sin? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 1846–1876

In one sentence: Sin is an offence against God and a failure in love; mortal sin destroys charity, venial sin wounds it, and God's mercy is greater than both.

What the Catechism says

The Gospel is the revelation of God's mercy to sinners. To receive it we must admit our faults. Sin is an offence against reason, truth and right conscience; a failure in genuine love for God and neighbour caused by a perverse attachment to certain goods.

Sins can be distinguished by their object, by the virtues they oppose, or by whether they concern God, neighbour or self. Mortal sin requires grave matter, full knowledge and deliberate consent; it destroys charity and, unrepented, leads to exclusion from the Kingdom. Venial sin weakens charity. Repeated sins produce vices; the capital sins are pride, avarice, envy, wrath, lust, gluttony and sloth. We can also cooperate in others' sins, and sin can create "structures of sin" in society.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 1846–1848 The Gospel is the revelation in Jesus Christ of God's mercy to sinners. "You shall call his name Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins." To receive his mercy, we must admit our faults. "If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves." Grace must uncover sin so as to convert our hearts: "Where sin increased, grace abounded all the more."

§ 1849–1853 Sin is an offence against reason, truth and right conscience; it is failure in genuine love for God and neighbour caused by a perverse attachment to certain goods. It wounds human nature and injures human solidarity. It has been defined as "an utterance, a deed, or a desire contrary to the eternal law" (Augustine). Sin is an offence against God; it sets itself against God's love and turns our hearts away from it. Like the first sin, it is disobedience, a revolt against God through the will to become "like gods". In the Passion, where Christ's mercy vanquishes it, sin most clearly manifests its violence and its many forms. Sins can be distinguished according to their objects, the virtues they oppose, or the commandments they violate; by whether they concern God, neighbour or oneself; and as spiritual or carnal, in thought, word, deed or omission. The root of sin is in the heart of man.

§ 1854–1864 The gravity of sins: mortal and venial. Mortal sin destroys charity in the heart by a grave violation of God's law; it turns man away from God. Venial sin allows charity to subsist, even though it offends and wounds it. For a sin to be mortal, three conditions must together be met: grave matter (specified by the Ten Commandments), full knowledge, and deliberate consent. Unintentional ignorance can diminish or even remove imputability; feigned ignorance and hardness of heart do not. The promptings of feelings and passions, and external pressures or pathological disorders, can also diminish the voluntary and free character of the offence. Mortal sin, unrepented, causes exclusion from Christ's kingdom and the eternal death of hell, for our freedom has the power to make choices for ever. However, although we can judge that an act is in itself a grave offence, we must entrust judgement of persons to the justice and mercy of God. Venial sin weakens charity, manifests a disordered affection for created goods, impedes progress in virtue, and merits temporal punishment; deliberate and unrepented venial sin disposes us little by little to commit mortal sin. Blasphemy against the Spirit: there are no limits to God's mercy, but anyone who deliberately refuses to accept his mercy by repenting rejects forgiveness.

§ 1865–1869 The proliferation of sin: sin creates a proclivity to sin; it engenders vice by repetition. Vices can be classified according to the virtues they oppose, or linked to the capital sins: pride, avarice, envy, wrath, lust, gluttony, and sloth or acedia. Sins that "cry to heaven": the blood of Abel, the sin of the Sodomites, the cry of the oppressed people in Egypt, the cry of the foreigner, the widow and the orphan, injustice to the wage earner. We have responsibility for sins committed by others when we cooperate in them: participating directly and voluntarily, ordering, advising, praising or approving them, not disclosing or hindering them when obliged to, or protecting evil-doers. Sins give rise to social situations and institutions contrary to divine goodness: "structures of sin", an expression and effect of personal sins.

Words to know

Mortal sin
Sin of grave matter, committed with full knowledge and deliberate consent.
Capital sins
Seven vices that give rise to other sins: pride, avarice, envy, wrath, lust, gluttony, sloth (acedia).

From the Fathers and saints

  • “Sin is a word, deed or desire contrary to the eternal law.” (St Augustine (Against Faustus 22.27))
  • “Pride is the queen of vices; when she has conquered the heart she hands it over to the seven principal vices, her generals.” (St Gregory the Great (Moralia 31.45))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Any sin about a grave matter is automatically mortal.
    What the Church teaches: Mortal sin requires grave matter, full knowledge and deliberate consent together (§ 1857).
  • Not quite: We can know that a particular person is in hell.
    What the Church teaches: We may judge acts, but judgement of persons belongs to God's justice and mercy (§ 1861).
  • Not quite: Venial sins don't matter.
    What the Church teaches: They wound charity, impede virtue, and, deliberate and unrepented, dispose us toward mortal sin (§ 1863).

Psychology connection

The distinction between guilt ("I did something bad") and shame ("I am bad") matters here. Meta-analytic work associates shame-proneness, more than guilt-proneness, with depression and other symptoms (Kim, Thibodeau & Jorgensen 2011, Psychological Bulletin). The Catechism's order, mercy first and then sin, supports healthy guilt that leads to repair rather than global self-condemnation.

To think about

Which capital sin is the root of most of your struggles? Pray for the opposite virtue.

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand sin as an offence against love, the conditions for mortal sin, the capital sins, and our share in others' sins and social structures.

  1. Why does the Catechism place sin after mercy (§ 1846)?
  2. Apply the three conditions for mortal sin to a real (anonymised) example.
  3. Which capital sin shows up most in our culture? In your life?

Activity: Capital sins and contrary virtues: match each capital sin to its remedy (pride–humility, avarice–generosity, envy–kindness, wrath–patience, lust–chastity, gluttony–temperance, sloth–diligence) and plan one act of the opposite virtue.
