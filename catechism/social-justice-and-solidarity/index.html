Social justice and solidarity

What does the Catholic Church teach about social justice and solidarity? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 1928–1948

In one sentence: Social justice flows from the dignity of every person; differences are a call to share, and solidarity is a Christian virtue.

What the Catechism says

Society ensures social justice when it provides conditions allowing associations and individuals to obtain what is their due. Social justice can only be achieved in respect for the transcendent dignity of each person. Everyone must see their neighbour as "another self", above all the poor and the excluded.

All people share the same nature, origin and dignity, so every form of discrimination is to be overcome; yet differences of talents are part of God's plan, so that we need and share with one another. Sinful inequalities contradict the Gospel. Solidarity, or "friendship", is a direct demand of human and Christian brotherhood, expressed in the fair distribution of goods and pay for work.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 1928–1933 Society ensures social justice when it provides the conditions that allow associations or individuals to obtain what is their due. Social justice can be obtained only in respecting the transcendent dignity of man. Respect for the human person entails respect for the rights that flow from his dignity; these rights are prior to society and must be recognised by it. Respect for the human person proceeds by way of respect for the principle that "everyone should look upon his neighbour (without any exception) as 'another self'". The duty of making ourselves a neighbour to others and actively serving them becomes even more urgent when it involves the disadvantaged. This same duty extends to those who think or act differently from us. The teaching of Christ goes so far as to require the forgiveness of offences; it extends the commandment of love to every enemy.

§ 1934–1938 Equality and differences: created in the image of the one God and equally endowed with rational souls, all men have the same nature and the same origin; all therefore enjoy an equal dignity. Every form of social or cultural discrimination in fundamental personal rights on the grounds of sex, race, colour, social conditions, language, or religion must be curbed and eradicated as incompatible with God's design. On coming into the world, man is not equipped with everything he needs: differences appear tied to age, physical abilities, intellectual or moral aptitudes, and the distribution of wealth. These differences belong to God's plan, who wills that each receive what he needs from others, and that those with particular talents share the benefits with those who need them. There also exist sinful inequalities that affect millions of men and women; they are in open contradiction of the Gospel.

§ 1939–1942 Human solidarity: the principle of solidarity, also articulated in terms of "friendship" or "social charity", is a direct demand of human and Christian brotherhood. Solidarity is manifested in the first place by the distribution of goods and remuneration for work. It presupposes the effort for a more just social order where tensions can be better resolved and conflicts settled by negotiation. Socio-economic problems can be resolved only with the help of all forms of solidarity: of the poor among themselves, between rich and poor, of workers among themselves, between employers and employees, among nations and peoples. Solidarity is also a Christian virtue: it practises the sharing of spiritual goods even more than material ones.

Words to know

Solidarity
Firm commitment to the common good, because we are all responsible for all.

In the Bible

  • Matt 25:40: as long as you did it to one of these my least brethren
  • Gal 3:28: neither Jew nor Greek

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST II-II q.58 a.6: Legal (general) justice directs every virtue towards the common good.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “Solidarity is not a feeling of vague compassion… it is a firm and persevering determination to commit oneself to the common good.” (St John Paul II (Sollicitudo Rei Socialis 38, 1987))
  • “I could well have supplied each of you with all your needs… but I wanted to make you dependent on one another, so that you would be my ministers to share the graces I have given you.” (St Catherine of Siena (Dialogue 7))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Social justice is a political ideology foreign to the Church.
    What the Church teaches: The Catechism defines social justice as giving people what is their due, grounded in human dignity (§§ 1928–1930).
  • Not quite: All inequality is unjust.
    What the Church teaches: Some differences belong to God's plan and call for sharing; others are sinful inequalities that contradict the Gospel (§§ 1936–1938).
