The battle of prayer

What does the Catholic Church teach about battle of prayer? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 2725–2758

In one sentence: Prayer is a battle against ourselves and the tempter: distractions, dryness and discouragement are overcome by humility, trust and perseverance.

What the Catechism says

Prayer is both a gift of grace and a determined response on our part: it always presupposes effort. Objections come from false ideas of prayer (that it is psychology, or useless), from the world's values, and from our failures. The difficulties: distraction (turn back to the Lord humbly), dryness (stay faithful, like the grain of wheat), and acedia, a form of depression due to lax asceticism.

We may feel our prayer isn't heard. But God knows what we need; we should ask for the Spirit, trust as children, and persevere, praying always. "Pray constantly." Jesus' own prayer at the Last Supper, the priestly prayer (John 17), embraces the whole economy of salvation.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 2725–2728 Prayer is both a gift of grace and a determined response on our part. It always presupposes effort. The great figures of prayer of the Old Covenant before Christ, as well as the Mother of God, the saints and Jesus himself, all teach us this: prayer is a battle. Against whom? Against ourselves and against the wiles of the tempter who does all he can to turn man away from prayer. We pray as we live, because we live as we pray. Objections to prayer: erroneous notions (prayer as psychological activity, or effort of concentration to reach a mental void, or ritual words and postures); mentalities of "this world" (only what is verifiable is true; productivity; sensuality and comfort; activism that sees prayer as a flight from the world); and discouragement during periods of dryness, sadness that we have not given everything, disappointment over not being heard according to our own will, wounded pride, our resistance to the idea that prayer is a free and unmerited gift.

§ 2729–2733 Humble vigilance of heart: the habitual difficulty in prayer is distraction. To set about hunting down distractions would be to fall into their trap, when all that is necessary is to turn back to our heart: a distraction reveals to us what we are attached to, and this humble awareness before the Lord should awaken our preferential love for him. Another difficulty, especially for those sincerely wanting to pray, is dryness: when the heart is separated from God, with no taste for thoughts, memories and feelings, even spiritual ones. This is the moment of sheer faith clinging faithfully to Jesus in his agony and in his tomb. "Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies… it bears much fruit." The most common, yet most hidden, temptation is our lack of faith, revealed by our actual preferences. Acedia: a form of depression stemming from lax ascetical practice, decreasing vigilance and carelessness of heart. "The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak."

§ 2734–2741 Filial trust: filial trust is tested when we feel that our prayer is not always heard. Why do we complain of not being heard? "You ask and do not receive, because you ask wrongly." If we ask with a divided heart, God cannot answer. Our prayer is efficacious because it is united in faith with that of Jesus. "Do not be troubled if you do not immediately receive from God what you ask him; for he desires to do something even greater for you, while you cling to him in prayer" (Evagrius). "God wills that our desire should be exercised in prayer, that we may be able to receive what he is prepared to give" (Augustine).

§ 2742–2758 Persevering in love: "Pray constantly… always and for everything giving thanks." "It is possible to offer fervent prayer even while walking in public or strolling alone, or seated in your shop… or while busy at your cooking" (Chrysostom). Prayer and Christian life are inseparable, for they concern the same love and the same renunciation. "He 'prays without ceasing' who unites prayer to works and good works to prayer" (Origen). The prayer of the hour of Jesus: the priestly prayer of John 17, spoken at the hour of his Passover, sums up the whole economy of creation and salvation, and inspires the great petitions of the Our Father.

Words to know

Acedia
Spiritual sloth: a disgust or listlessness about spiritual things.
Dryness
Prayer without felt consolation; a time of faithful perseverance.

In the Bible

  • 1 Thess 5:17: pray without ceasing
  • Luke 18:1-8: always to pray, and not to faint
  • John 17:1-26: the priestly prayer

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST II-II q.35 a.1: Acedia is sorrow about spiritual good, the good God offers us.
  • ST II-II q.83 a.13: Prayer can be meritorious even with involuntary distractions, if one's first intention was to pray.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “Do not be troubled if you do not immediately receive from God what you ask him; for he desires to do something even greater for you, while you cling to him in prayer.” (Evagrius Ponticus (On Prayer 34))
  • “It is possible to offer fervent prayer even while walking in public or strolling alone, or seated in your shop, while buying or selling, or even while cooking.” (St John Chrysostom (Ecloga de oratione 2))
  • “God wills that our desire should be exercised in prayer, that we may be able to receive what he is prepared to give.” (St Augustine (Letter 130, to Proba, 8.17))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: If prayer is hard, I'm not meant to pray.
    What the Church teaches: Prayer is a battle for everyone, including the saints and Jesus himself (§ 2725).
  • Not quite: Distractions ruin prayer; I must eliminate them first.
    What the Church teaches: Hunting distractions is a trap; turning back to the Lord is enough, and distractions show what we are attached to (§ 2729).
  • Not quite: Unanswered prayer means God isn't listening.
    What the Church teaches: God may wish to give something greater; we must ask with an undivided heart, in union with Jesus' prayer (§§ 2735–2741).

Psychology connection

The Catechism distinguishes spiritual acedia from illness. Persistent low mood, loss of interest and exhaustion can also be clinical depression, which deserves professional care; spiritual guidance and medical help work together, not against each other.

§ 2733 calls acedia "a form of depression" stemming from spiritual laxity. Clinically it is important not to confuse the two: acedia is a spiritual condition of the will, whereas major depressive disorder (DSM-5-TR) is an illness with low mood or loss of interest for at least two weeks plus symptoms such as sleep and appetite change, guilt, and difficulty concentrating. Depression can make prayer feel dry or impossible; it is not a spiritual failure and calls for proper treatment. Spiritual direction and clinical care can work together.

To think about

What is your biggest obstacle in prayer: distraction, dryness or busyness? Make one plan against it.

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand prayer as a battle, recognise the objections and difficulties (distraction, dryness, acedia, unanswered prayer), and resolve to persevere.

  1. Which objection to prayer in § 2727 do you hear most from friends?
  2. How do you usually deal with distractions? What does § 2729 suggest instead?
  3. Have you experienced "unanswered" prayer? How does § 2737 help?

Activity: Prayer battle plan: name your biggest obstacle (time, distraction, dryness, phone), and choose one concrete remedy (a fixed time, a set place, the phone in another room, a short prayer when you notice distraction). Review in two weeks.
