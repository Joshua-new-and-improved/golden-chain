The Beatitudes and our desire for happiness

What does the Catholic Church teach about Beatitudes and our desire for happiness? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 1716–1729

In one sentence: God put a desire for happiness in every heart, and only he can satisfy it; the Beatitudes describe that happiness and the way to it.

What the Catechism says

The Beatitudes are at the heart of Jesus' preaching. They depict his own face and his charity; they express the vocation of the faithful; and they shed light on the actions and attitudes of the Christian life.

The desire for happiness is of divine origin: God has placed it in the human heart in order to draw us to himself, who alone can satisfy it. Christian beatitude means seeing God, entering into his joy and rest. It purifies our hearts of bad instincts and teaches that true happiness is not in riches, fame, power, science or any creature, but in God alone.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 1716–1717 The Beatitudes are at the heart of Jesus' preaching. They take up the promises made to the chosen people since Abraham, and fulfil them by ordering them no longer merely to the possession of a territory but to the Kingdom of heaven. They depict the countenance of Jesus Christ and portray his charity; they express the vocation of the faithful associated with the glory of his Passion and Resurrection; they shed light on the actions and attitudes characteristic of the Christian life; they are the paradoxical promises that sustain hope in the midst of tribulations.

§ 1718–1719 The desire for happiness is of divine origin: God has placed it in the human heart in order to draw man to the One who alone can fulfil it. "How is it, then, that I seek you, Lord? Since in seeking you, my God, I seek a happy life" (Augustine). The Beatitudes reveal the goal of human existence, the ultimate end of human acts: God calls us to his own beatitude.

§ 1720–1724 The New Testament uses several expressions for this beatitude: the coming of the Kingdom, the vision of God, entering into the joy of the Lord, entering into God's rest. God has put us in the world to know, serve and love him, and so to come to paradise. Beatitude makes us "partakers of the divine nature" and of eternal life. It surpasses the understanding and powers of man; it comes from an entirely free gift of God: supernatural. The beatitude we are promised confronts us with decisive moral choices: it invites us to purify our hearts of bad instincts and to seek the love of God above all else. It teaches us that true happiness is not found in riches or well-being, in human fame or power, nor in any human achievement, however beneficial, but in God alone, the source of every good and of all love.

Words to know

Beatitude
Blessedness: the happiness of seeing God and sharing his life.

In the Bible

  • Matt 5:1-12: the Beatitudes
  • 2 Pet 1:4: partakers of the divine nature

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST I-II q.2 a.8: Happiness cannot consist in any created good, but only in God.
  • ST I-II q.69 a.1: The Beatitudes are acts flowing from the virtues and gifts, which are rewarded with beatitude.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “How is it, then, that I seek you, Lord? Since in seeking you, my God, I seek a happy life, let me seek you so that my soul may live.” (St Augustine (Confessions X.20.29))
  • “Nothing can satisfy man's will except the universal good, which is found not in any creature but in God alone.” (St Thomas Aquinas (ST I-II q.2 a.8))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: The Beatitudes are nice ideals but not really moral teaching.
    What the Church teaches: They are the heart of Jesus' preaching and the charter of Christian life (§§ 1716–1717).
  • Not quite: Wanting to be happy is selfish.
    What the Church teaches: The desire for happiness is of divine origin; God placed it in us to draw us to himself (§ 1718).

Psychology connection

Positive psychology distinguishes hedonic wellbeing (pleasure) from eudaimonic wellbeing (meaning, purpose, growth); meta-analytic work links purpose in life to lower mortality and better mental health (Cohen, Bavishi & Rozanski 2016, Psychosomatic Medicine). Aquinas's account of beatitude is eudaimonic: happiness is the possession of the highest good, not a feeling.
