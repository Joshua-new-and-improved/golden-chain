The canon, and the two Testaments

What does the Catholic Church teach about canon, and the two Testaments? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 120–141

In one sentence: The Church discerned the canon: 46 books of the Old Testament and 27 of the New; both Testaments are God's word, and they explain each other.

What the Catechism says

Apostolic Tradition led the Church to recognise which books belong in Scripture: 46 in the Old Testament and 27 in the New. The Old Testament is indispensable: its books are inspired and keep their lasting value, preparing for Christ and teaching about God and humanity. The Gospels are the heart of Scripture, because they are the main witness to Jesus.

The two Testaments are a unity: the New lies hidden in the Old, and the Old is unveiled in the New (St Augustine). The Church urges all the faithful to read Scripture often, because "ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ" (St Jerome).

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 120 It was by apostolic Tradition that the Church discerned which writings are to be included in the list of the sacred books. This complete list is called the canon: 46 (or 45, if Jeremiah and Lamentations are counted as one) for the Old Testament and 27 for the New.

§ 121–123 The Old Testament is an indispensable part of Scripture. Its books are divinely inspired and retain a permanent value, for the Old Covenant has never been revoked. Its economy was oriented to prepare for the coming of Christ; though it contains matters imperfect and provisional, it bears witness to the whole divine pedagogy of God's saving love. Christians venerate it as true Word of God; the Church has always rejected the idea (Marcionism) that it should be discarded because the New has rendered it void.

§ 124–127 The New Testament, whose central object is Jesus Christ, hands on the ultimate truth of God's Revelation. The Gospels are the heart of all the Scriptures, as the principal witness to the life and teaching of Jesus. They were formed in three stages: the life and teaching of Jesus; the oral tradition of the apostles; and the written Gospels, composed by the evangelists, who selected, synthesised and explained, always telling the honest truth about Jesus. The fourfold Gospel holds a unique place in the Church's liturgy and the lives of the saints.

§ 128–130 The unity of the two Testaments: the Church, from apostolic times, has illuminated this unity by typology, which discerns in God's works of the Old Covenant prefigurations of what he accomplished in Christ. Christians therefore read the Old Testament in the light of Christ crucified and risen; yet it keeps its own value. As St Augustine said, the New lies hidden in the Old and the Old is unveiled in the New.

§ 131–133 Scripture in the life of the Church: such is the force and power of the Word of God that it is the support and energy of the Church and the food of the soul. The study of the sacred page should be the very soul of theology; preaching, catechesis and all Christian instruction should be nourished by it. The Church forcefully exhorts all the faithful to learn the surpassing knowledge of Christ by frequent reading of the divine Scriptures: "Ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ" (St Jerome).

§ 134–141 In Brief: all Scripture is one book, Christ; God is its author; it must be interpreted in the Church, attending to the literal and spiritual senses; the Church accepts 46 Old and 27 New Testament books; the four Gospels occupy a central place; the two Testaments form a unity; and the Church honours the Scriptures as she honours the Lord's Body.

  • “The New Testament lies hidden in the Old; the Old is made manifest in the New.” (St Augustine (Questions on the Heptateuch 2.73))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: The Old Testament is just for Jews, and Christians can ignore it.
    What the Church teaches: It is an indispensable part of Scripture, never to be discarded (§§ 121–123).
  • Not quite: Catholics added books to the Bible.
    What the Church teaches: The Church's canon (with the deuterocanonical books) was recognised from the early centuries and confirmed at Trent; it follows the Greek Old Testament used by the apostles.
  • Not quite: The Gospels are myths invented much later.
    What the Church teaches: They arose from Jesus' life, the apostles' preaching, and the evangelists' faithful writing (§ 126).

To think about

Catholic Bibles have seven more Old Testament books than most Protestant Bibles. Which do you know? (Tobit, Judith, 1–2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch.)

For catechists

Aim: Participants know the canon, the place of the Gospels, the unity of the Testaments, and are encouraged to read Scripture daily.

  1. Which Old Testament stories can you connect to Christ (typology)?
  2. What are the three stages in the formation of the Gospels?
  3. What helps or hinders you from reading Scripture every day?

Activity: Typology match: pair Old Testament events (Passover lamb, manna, Jonah in the fish, the bronze serpent) with New Testament fulfilments (the Cross, the Eucharist, the Resurrection, John 3:14).
