The Eighth Commandment

What does the Catholic Church teach about Eighth Commandment? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 2464–2513

In one sentence: Live in the truth: no lies, false witness, detraction or calumny; respect others' reputation, and use the media responsibly.

What the Catechism says

God is the source of all truth; Jesus is the Truth. The disciple is called to live in the truth, with simplicity of life, and to bear witness to it, even to martyrdom.

The commandment forbids false witness and perjury, rash judgement, detraction (revealing others' faults without valid reason), calumny (lies harming reputation), flattery, boasting, irony aimed at belittling, and lying: speaking a falsehood with the intention of deceiving. Damage must be repaired. Not everyone has the right to every truth: secrets and professional confidences must be kept, and the seal of confession is inviolable. Media must serve truth and the common good. Truth is also expressed in beauty, especially sacred art.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 2464–2470 "You shall not bear false witness against your neighbour." The eighth commandment forbids misrepresenting the truth in our relations with others. Living in the truth: the Old Testament attests that God is the source of all truth. In Jesus Christ the whole of God's truth has been made manifest: "I am the truth." Truthfulness is the virtue which consists in showing oneself true in deeds and truthful in words, and guarding against duplicity, dissimulation and hypocrisy. Men could not live with one another if there were not mutual confidence that they were being truthful. Truthfulness keeps to the just mean between what ought to be expressed and what ought to be kept secret: it entails honesty and discretion.

§ 2471–2474 Bearing witness to the truth: before Pilate, Christ proclaims that he "has come into the world, to bear witness to the truth". The Christian is not to "be ashamed of testifying to our Lord". Martyrdom is the supreme witness given to the truth of the faith: a witness even unto death. The Church has painstakingly collected the records of those who persevered to the end in witnessing to their faith: the acts of the martyrs.

§ 2475–2487 Offences against truth: false witness and perjury (a lie under oath) are particularly grave in court. Respect for the reputation of persons forbids rash judgement (assuming as true, without sufficient foundation, the moral fault of a neighbour), detraction (disclosing another's faults and failings to persons who did not know them, without objectively valid reason), and calumny (remarks contrary to the truth which harm the reputation of others). Every disciple should interpret a neighbour's words and deeds in a favourable way if possible (St Ignatius). Flattery, adulation or complaisance are forbidden when they encourage and confirm another in malicious acts. Boasting or bragging is an offence against truth; so is irony aimed at disparaging someone. Lying is the most direct offence against the truth: "speaking a falsehood with the intention of deceiving". By its very nature it is to be condemned. The gravity of a lie is measured by the nature of the truth it deforms, the circumstances, the intentions and the harm suffered by its victims. Every offence against justice and truth entails the duty of reparation, even if its author has been forgiven.

§ 2488–2492 Respect for the truth: the right to the communication of the truth is not unconditional. Charity and respect for the truth should dictate the response to every request for information. No one is bound to reveal the truth to someone who does not have the right to know it. The sacramental seal is inviolable. Professional secrets (of politicians, soldiers, doctors, lawyers) or confidential information given under the seal of secrecy must be kept, except in exceptional cases where keeping the secret is bound to cause very grave harm to the one who confided it, to the one who received it, or to a third party. Everyone should observe an appropriate reserve concerning persons' private lives.

§ 2493–2513 Use of the social communications media: information through the media is at the service of the common good; society has a right to information based on truth, freedom, justice and solidarity. Media users should practise moderation and discipline. Journalists should not offend against charity in disseminating information, and should avoid defamation. Truth, beauty and sacred art: truth is beautiful in itself. Art is a distinctively human form of expression; it bears a certain likeness to God's activity in creation. Sacred art is true and beautiful when its form corresponds to its particular vocation: evoking and glorifying, in faith and adoration, the transcendent mystery of God.

Words to know

Detraction
Revealing another's real faults to those who didn't know, without objectively valid reason.
Calumny
Harming another's reputation by lies.

In the Bible

  • Exod 20:16: thou shalt not bear false witness
  • John 14:6: I am the way, and the truth
  • Eph 4:25: putting away lying, speak ye the truth

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST II-II q.110 a.3: Every lie is a sin, because words are naturally signs of thought; to say what one doesn't think abuses speech.
  • ST II-II q.73 a.2: Backbiting is grave because it takes away a person's good name, a great good.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “A lie consists in speaking a falsehood with the intention of deceiving.” (St Augustine (On Lying 4.5))
  • “Every good Christian ought to be more ready to give a favourable interpretation to another's statement than to condemn it.” (St Ignatius of Loyola (Spiritual Exercises 22))
  • “Let me be food for the wild beasts, through whom I can reach God. I am God's wheat.” (St Ignatius of Antioch (Letter to the Romans 4.1))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Only big lies matter.
    What the Church teaches: Lying is by its nature to be condemned; gravity varies with the truth deformed, intent and harm (§§ 2484–2485).
  • Not quite: Gossip is fine if what I say is true.
    What the Church teaches: Disclosing someone's faults without valid reason is detraction, a sin even when true (§ 2477).
  • Not quite: Keeping a confidence is always lying by omission.
    What the Church teaches: No one is bound to reveal truth to someone without a right to know; professional secrets and confidences must generally be kept (§§ 2489–2491).

Psychology connection

Confidentiality in § 2491 closely parallels the ethics of psychologists. The HPCSA's ethical rules for psychology (Annexure 12 to the Ethical Rules of Conduct) require psychologists to keep confidences except where disclosure is required by law or needed to protect someone from serious harm: the same "exceptional cases" logic the Catechism uses.

To think about

Spend one day this week without exaggerating, flattering or gossiping. What did you notice?

For catechists

Aim: Participants grow in truthfulness and understand witness, the offences against truth and reputation (including detraction and calumny), confidentiality, and the ethics of media.

  1. What is the difference between detraction and calumny? Which is more common in WhatsApp groups?
  2. How do Ignatius's words ("interpret favourably") change how we read others' posts?
  3. When is keeping a secret right, and when must it be broken (§ 2491)?

Activity: A one-week "speech fast": no gossip, no exaggeration, no sarcasm aimed at others. At the next session, share what was difficult and what changed.
