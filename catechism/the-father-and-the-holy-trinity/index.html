The Father, and the Holy Trinity

What does the Catholic Church teach about Father, and the Holy Trinity? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 232–267

In one sentence: The central mystery of the faith: one God in three Persons, Father, Son and Holy Spirit.

What the Catechism says

Christians are baptised in the name (singular) of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. The Trinity is the central mystery of Christian faith and life: God's own inner life, which we could never have known unless he revealed it.

Jesus reveals God as Father in a new sense: Father of his eternal Son. The Spirit is revealed as another Person, proceeding from the Father and the Son. The Church's councils used words like "substance", "person" and "relation" to say it precisely: we do not believe in three gods, but one God in three Persons, really distinct by their relations of origin. All God's works are common to the three, yet each shows what is proper to him. The goal of everything is that we enter the Trinity's perfect unity.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 232–237 Christians are baptised in the name (not "names") of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. The Trinity is the central mystery of Christian faith and life: God's own inner life, the source of all other mysteries, and the light that illumines them. It is a mystery in the strict sense, one that could never be known unless revealed. God's works ("economy") reveal who he is in himself ("theology"), and who he is in himself illuminates his works.

§ 238–242 Many religions call God "Father" as creator, or for his care. But Jesus revealed that God is Father in an unheard-of sense: not only as Creator, but eternally Father in relation to his only Son, who is eternally Son only in relation to the Father. So the apostles confess Jesus to be the Word, the image of the invisible God. Following this tradition, the Council of Nicaea (325) confessed that the Son is "consubstantial" (homoousios) with the Father: one only God with him.

§ 243–248 Before his Passover Jesus announced "another Paraclete", the Holy Spirit, who is a divine Person distinct from Jesus and the Father. The Spirit's eternal origin is revealed in his mission: he is sent by the Father in the name of the Son, and by the Son from the Father. The Council of Constantinople (381) confessed the Spirit as "the Lord, the giver of life", worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son. The Latin tradition says the Spirit proceeds from the Father "and the Son" (filioque); the Eastern tradition says "from the Father through the Son". Rightly understood, these express the same faith.

§ 249–256 The Church worked out her Trinitarian faith over the first centuries, borrowing terms from philosophy. "Substance" (essence, nature) names the divine being in its unity; "person" (hypostasis) names Father, Son and Spirit in their real distinction; "relation" names the fact that their distinction lies only in their relations to one another. The Trinity is one: not three gods, but one God in three Persons, each wholly God. The Persons are really distinct: Father begets, Son is begotten, Spirit proceeds. And they are relative to one another: their distinction does not divide the divine unity.

§ 257–260 The whole divine economy (creation, salvation, sanctification) is the common work of the three Persons, for they have one and the same nature and operation. Yet each shows what is proper to him: the Father, from whom all things come; the Son, through whom; the Spirit, in whom. The ultimate goal of the whole divine economy is that creatures enter into the perfect unity of the Blessed Trinity; even now we are called to be a dwelling for it.

§ 261–267 In Brief: the mystery of the Trinity is the central mystery of faith; the Incarnation and the sending of the Spirit reveal it; the Church confesses one God in three Persons, consubstantial; the divine Persons are inseparable in what they are and in what they do; and by Baptism we are called to share in the life of the Trinity, here in faith and after death in eternal light.

Words to know

Consubstantial
Of one and the same substance: the Son is God as the Father is God.
Person
In God, a distinct "who": Father, Son and Spirit, distinguished by their relations.

In the Bible

  • Matt 28:19: in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost
  • John 14:16-17: another Paraclete
  • 2 Cor 13:13: the grace of our Lord

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST I q.29 a.4: "Person" in God signifies a relation as subsisting.
  • ST I q.32 a.1: The Trinity cannot be proven by reason; arguments for it only show it is not impossible.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “I cannot think of the One without being enlightened by the splendour of the Three; I cannot distinguish the Three without being carried back to the One.” (St Gregory of Nazianzus (Oration 40.41))
  • “O my God, Trinity whom I adore, help me to forget myself entirely so as to dwell in you.” (St Elizabeth of the Trinity (prayer, 1904))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: The Trinity is like one person wearing three masks (Father in the Old Testament, Son in the Gospels, Spirit now).
    What the Church teaches: This is the ancient error of modalism; the three Persons are really distinct, always (§ 254).
  • Not quite: The Son and Spirit are lesser, created beings.
    What the Church teaches: Each Person is wholly God, consubstantial with the Father (§§ 242, 253).
  • Not quite: The Trinity can be proven by reason, or is a mathematical contradiction.
    What the Church teaches: It is a mystery known only by revelation, beyond reason but not against it: one in nature, three in persons (§ 237).

To think about

Next time you make the Sign of the Cross, notice that you name three Persons and one Name.
