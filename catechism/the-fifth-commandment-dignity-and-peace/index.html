The Fifth Commandment: dignity and peace

What does the Catholic Church teach about Fifth Commandment: dignity and peace? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 2284–2330

In one sentence: Respect for persons includes not leading others into sin, caring for health, respecting the body, and working for peace.

What the Catechism says

Scandal, an attitude or behaviour that leads another to sin, is grave when it comes from those who teach or lead. Life and health are gifts: we should take reasonable care of them, while rejecting a cult of the body. Drug use (except for strictly therapeutic reasons), abuse of food, alcohol or medicine, and reckless driving offend against this commandment.

Scientific research must respect the person: experiments without consent, organ trafficking and degrading treatment are forbidden. The dead must be treated with respect. Jesus calls us to peace, the tranquillity of order. Anger and hatred desiring another's evil are sins against charity. War must be avoided by every means; the strict conditions of legitimate defence by military force are the "just war" criteria.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 2284–2287 Respect for the souls of others: scandal is an attitude or behaviour which leads another to do evil. The person who gives scandal becomes his neighbour's tempter. Scandal is grave when given by those who by nature or office are obliged to teach and educate others. "Whoever causes one of these little ones who believe in me to sin, it would be better for him to have a great millstone fastened round his neck and to be drowned." Scandal can be provoked by laws or institutions, by fashion or opinion.

§ 2288–2291 Respect for health: life and physical health are precious gifts entrusted to us by God. We must take reasonable care of them, taking into account the needs of others and the common good. Society should help attain living conditions that allow people to grow and reach maturity: food and clothing, housing, health care, basic education, employment and social assistance. The virtue of temperance disposes us to avoid every kind of excess: the abuse of food, alcohol, tobacco or medicine. Those incur grave guilt who, by drunkenness or a love of speed, endanger their own and others' safety on the road, at sea or in the air. The use of drugs inflicts very grave damage on human health and life; except on strictly therapeutic grounds, it is a grave offence. Clandestine production of and trafficking in drugs are scandalous practices.

§ 2292–2301 Respect for the person and scientific research: scientific, medical or psychological experiments on human individuals or groups can contribute to healing the sick and advancing public health, but research must respect the person and the moral law. Research or experimentation on the human being cannot legitimate acts contrary to the dignity of persons; free and informed consent is required. Organ transplants are in conformity with the moral law if the physical and psychological dangers and risks to the donor are proportionate to the good sought; organ donation after death is a noble and meritorious act. Respect for bodily integrity: kidnapping and hostage-taking, terrorism, and torture are gravely against justice and charity. Except when performed for strictly therapeutic medical reasons, directly intended amputations, mutilations and sterilisations of innocent persons are against the moral law. Respect for the dead: the bodies of the dead must be treated with respect and charity, in faith and hope of the Resurrection; cremation is permitted provided it does not demonstrate a denial of faith in the resurrection of the body.

§ 2302–2317 Safeguarding peace: Jesus recalls "You shall not kill" and forbids anger, which is a desire for revenge. To desire vengeance in order to do evil to someone is illicit, but it is praiseworthy to impose restitution "to correct vices and maintain justice". Deliberate hatred is contrary to charity. Peace is not merely the absence of war; it is "the tranquillity of order" (Augustine), the work of justice and the effect of charity. Earthly peace is the image and fruit of the peace of Christ. Avoiding war: all citizens and governments are obliged to work for the avoidance of war. The strict conditions for legitimate defence by military force ("just war" doctrine): the damage inflicted by the aggressor must be lasting, grave and certain; all other means must have been shown impractical or ineffective; there must be serious prospects of success; the use of arms must not produce evils graver than the evil to be eliminated. The evaluation belongs to the prudential judgement of those responsible for the common good. Conscientious objectors should be provided for. The moral law remains valid during armed conflict; non-combatants, wounded soldiers and prisoners must be respected; genocide and indiscriminate destruction of cities are crimes against God and man. The arms race does not ensure peace; injustice, excessive economic or social inequalities, envy, distrust and pride threaten peace and cause wars.

Words to know

Scandal
Leading another to sin by attitude or behaviour.
Just war criteria
Strict conditions for legitimate defence by force: lasting grave damage, other means ineffective, serious prospect of success, no graver evils produced.

In the Bible

  • Matt 18:6: whosoever shall scandalise one of these little ones
  • Matt 5:21-24: whosoever is angry with his brother
  • Matt 5:9: blessed are the peacemakers

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST II-II q.40 a.1: Thomas's conditions for a just war: legitimate authority, a just cause, and right intention.
  • ST II-II q.29 a.1: Peace is "the tranquillity of order" (Augustine).

From the Fathers and saints

  • “Peace is the tranquillity of order.” (St Augustine (City of God XIX.13))
  • “Lord, make me an instrument of your peace.” (St Francis of Assisi (attributed, Peace Prayer))
  • “He who is not angry when he has cause to be, sins… for unreasonable patience is the hotbed of many vices.” (St Thomas Aquinas (ST II-II q.158 a.8, citing St John Chrysostom))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: The fifth commandment only forbids murder.
    What the Church teaches: It also covers scandal, health, drugs, research ethics, bodily integrity, respect for the dead, anger, hatred and war (§§ 2284–2317).
  • Not quite: Catholics may not be cremated.
    What the Church teaches: Cremation is permitted if it does not express denial of the resurrection; ashes should be kept in a sacred place (§ 2301).
  • Not quite: Peace simply means no war.
    What the Church teaches: Peace is the tranquillity of order, the work of justice and the effect of charity (§ 2304).

Psychology connection

§ 2291's concern about drugs fits the evidence: substance-use disorders are among the leading causes of disability worldwide. Temperance has a psychological analogue in self-regulation research. Treatment works: for alcohol-use disorder, medications such as naltrexone and acamprosate have meta-analytic support (Jonas et al. 2014, JAMA), as do psychosocial treatments such as CBT and motivational interviewing. In South Africa, SANCA offers treatment and support.

To think about

Is there someone you are angry with? Pray for their good today.

For catechists

Aim: Participants grasp the wide scope of the fifth commandment: scandal, health and addiction, research ethics, bodily integrity, anger and peace, and the just war conditions.

  1. How can social media be a source of scandal (leading others into sin)?
  2. What does temperance look like with alcohol, food and screens in student life?
  3. Apply the just war conditions (§ 2309) to a current or historical conflict.

Activity: Peace practice: each person identifies one relationship where anger or resentment lingers and takes one concrete step toward peace this week (a prayer, a message, an apology).
