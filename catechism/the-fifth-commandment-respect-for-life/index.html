The Fifth Commandment: respect for life

What does the Catholic Church teach about Fifth Commandment: respect for life? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 2258–2283

In one sentence: Human life is sacred from conception to natural death; the deliberate killing of an innocent person is always gravely wrong.

What the Catechism says

Human life is sacred because from its beginning it involves God's creative action and remains in a special relationship with him. No one can claim the right directly to destroy an innocent human being. Legitimate defence is permitted, and can be a grave duty for those responsible for others. Regarding the death penalty, the Catechism (revised in 2018) teaches it is inadmissible.

Abortion and euthanasia are gravely contrary to the moral law. Human life must be protected from conception; embryos must be treated as persons. Suicide contradicts our natural inclination to life and love of self, neighbour and God; yet grave psychological disturbance, anguish or fear can diminish responsibility, and we should not despair of the salvation of those who have taken their own lives. The Church prays for them.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 2258–2262 "Human life is sacred because from its beginning it involves the creative action of God and it remains for ever in a special relationship with the Creator… God alone is the Lord of life from its beginning until its end: no one can under any circumstance claim for himself the right directly to destroy an innocent human being." Scripture tells of Cain's murder of Abel, showing the presence of anger and envy in man from the beginning. The covenant with Noah: "Whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed; for God made man in his own image." Jesus recalls "You shall not kill" and adds the prohibition of anger, hatred and vengeance, asking his disciples to love their enemies.

§ 2263–2267 Legitimate defence: the legitimate defence of persons and societies is not an exception to the prohibition of murder; it has a double effect: the preservation of one's own life, and the killing of the aggressor. "The one is intended, the other is not" (Aquinas). Love toward oneself remains a fundamental principle; legitimate defence can be not only a right but a grave duty for one responsible for the lives of others. The death penalty: in 2018 Pope Francis revised § 2267: "the Church teaches, in the light of the Gospel, that 'the death penalty is inadmissible because it is an attack on the inviolability and dignity of the person', and she works with determination for its abolition worldwide."

§ 2268–2269 Intentional homicide: the fifth commandment forbids direct and intentional killing as gravely sinful. Infanticide, fratricide, parricide and the murder of a spouse are especially grave. It forbids doing anything with the intention of indirectly bringing about a person's death, and exposing someone to mortal danger without grave reason, or refusing assistance to a person in danger. Unintentional killing is not morally imputable, but one is not exonerated from grave offence if, without proportionate reasons, one has acted in a way that brings about someone's death.

§ 2270–2275 Abortion: human life must be respected and protected absolutely from the moment of conception. From the first moment of his existence, a human being must be recognised as having the rights of a person, among which is the inviolable right of every innocent being to life. Since the first century the Church has affirmed the moral evil of every procured abortion: "You shall not kill the embryo by abortion" (Didache). Direct abortion, willed either as an end or a means, is gravely contrary to the moral law; formal cooperation in abortion incurs excommunication (latae sententiae). The inalienable right to life of every innocent individual is a constitutive element of civil society. The embryo must be treated from conception as a person; prenatal diagnosis is permissible when it respects life and is directed toward safeguarding or healing; producing embryos as disposable biological material is immoral.

§ 2276–2279 Euthanasia: those whose lives are diminished or weakened deserve special respect; sick or handicapped persons should be helped to lead lives as normal as possible. Whatever its motives and means, direct euthanasia (putting an end to the lives of handicapped, sick or dying persons) is morally unacceptable. Discontinuing medical procedures that are burdensome, dangerous, extraordinary or disproportionate to the expected outcome can be legitimate: it is the refusal of "over-zealous" treatment. The decisions should be made by the patient if competent, or by those legally entitled. Ordinary care owed to a sick person cannot be legitimately interrupted. Painkillers to alleviate the sufferings of the dying, even at the risk of shortening their days, can be morally in conformity with human dignity if death is not willed. Palliative care is a special form of disinterested charity.

§ 2280–2283 Suicide: everyone is responsible for his life before God who has given it. We are stewards, not owners. Suicide contradicts the natural inclination to preserve life and is gravely contrary to the just love of self, of neighbour and of God. Voluntary cooperation in suicide is contrary to the moral law. "Grave psychological disturbances, anguish, or grave fear of hardship, suffering, or torture can diminish the responsibility of the one committing suicide." "We should not despair of the eternal salvation of persons who have taken their own lives. By ways known to him alone, God can provide the opportunity for salutary repentance. The Church prays for persons who have taken their own lives."

Words to know

Legitimate defence
Protecting oneself or others, even if it causes the aggressor's death as an unintended effect.
Euthanasia
Ending the life of the sick or dying, by act or omission, to eliminate suffering.

In the Bible

  • Exod 20:13: thou shalt not kill
  • Gen 4:10: the voice of thy brother's blood
  • Ps 138:13-16 (139): thou hast protected me from my mother's womb

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST II-II q.64 a.7: Thomas's principle of double effect: self-defence is lawful when one intends to save one's life and the death of the aggressor is not intended.
  • ST II-II q.64 a.6: It is never lawful to kill the innocent.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “You shall not kill the embryo by abortion, nor cause the newborn to perish.” (Didache 2.2 (1st century))
  • “Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended… the act of self-defence may have two effects: the saving of one's life and the slaying of the aggressor.” (St Thomas Aquinas (ST II-II q.64 a.7))
  • “Love begins by taking care of the closest ones, the ones at home.” (St Teresa of Calcutta (attributed))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: The Church teaches that everyone who dies by suicide goes to hell.
    What the Church teaches: Psychological disturbance and anguish can diminish responsibility; we must not despair of their salvation, and the Church prays for them (§§ 2282–2283).
  • Not quite: Refusing aggressive treatment at the end of life is euthanasia.
    What the Church teaches: Discontinuing burdensome or disproportionate treatment can be legitimate; what is forbidden is intending death (§ 2278).
  • Not quite: The Catechism still allows the death penalty.
    What the Church teaches: Since 2018, § 2267 teaches that the death penalty is inadmissible (§ 2267, revised).

Psychology connection

CCC 2282 explicitly recognises that "grave psychological disturbances, anguish, or grave fear" can diminish responsibility: a clear point of contact with clinical understanding of suicidal crisis. The pastoral response is mercy, prayer and practical care.

§ 2282 recognises what psychology confirms: most people who die by suicide are suffering from a mental disorder, most often depression, and acute anguish narrows perception of options. Suicide is preventable; asking directly about suicidal thoughts does not increase risk (Dazzi et al. 2014, Psychological Medicine, systematic review). If you or someone you know is struggling, in South Africa contact the SADAG suicide crisis line, 0800 567 567, or speak to a doctor or priest.

To think about

Who in your community most needs life to be protected and cared for: the unborn, the sick, the elderly, the lonely?

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand the sanctity of life, legitimate defence, the revised teaching on the death penalty, abortion, euthanasia and suicide, with truth and compassion. Handle this session gently: some participants may have personal experience of abortion, suicide loss or end-of-life decisions.

  1. What does it mean that we are stewards, not owners, of our lives?
  2. How would you explain the difference between letting die and killing (§ 2278)?
  3. How can a parish support women in crisis pregnancies, and people grieving a suicide or an abortion?

Activity: Invite someone from a local pregnancy-care centre, hospice or bereavement ministry to speak about how they serve life practically. Share local help numbers with everyone (in South Africa, SADAG: 0800 567 567).
