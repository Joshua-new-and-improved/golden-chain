The First Commandment

What does the Catholic Church teach about First Commandment? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 2083–2141

In one sentence: Worship God alone: by faith, hope and charity, adoration and prayer; avoid idolatry, superstition and irreligion.

What the Catechism says

"I am the Lord your God… you shall have no other gods before me." The first commandment calls us to believe in God, hope in him and love him above all. It requires worship: adoration, prayer, sacrifice, promises and vows. It includes the right to religious freedom: no one may be forced to act against their conscience in religious matters.

It forbids superstition (attributing power to practices apart from the right interior attitude), idolatry (divinising what is not God: power, money, pleasure, the state), divination and magic (astrology, horoscopes, mediums), irreligion (sacrilege, simony), atheism and agnosticism. Veneration of sacred images is not idolatry, because honour paid to an image passes to the one it portrays.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 2083–2094 "I am the Lord your God… You shall have no other gods before me." The first commandment embraces faith, hope and charity. Faith: we must nourish and protect it with prudence and vigilance, and reject everything opposed to it. Sins against faith: voluntary doubt (disregarding or refusing to hold as true what God has revealed); incredulity; heresy (obstinate post-baptismal denial of a truth that must be believed); apostasy (total repudiation of the faith); schism (refusal of submission to the Pope or communion with the Church). Hope: sins against it are despair (ceasing to hope for salvation or forgiveness) and presumption (hoping to be saved without effort or conversion, or without God's help). Charity: sins against it are indifference, ingratitude, lukewarmness, acedia (spiritual sloth) and hatred of God.

§ 2095–2109 The virtue of religion disposes us to give God what is his due. Adoration is the first act of religion: acknowledging God as Creator and Saviour, Lord and Master of everything that exists. Prayer: lifting the spirit to God is an expression of adoration. Sacrifice: outward sacrifice must express spiritual sacrifice; "the sacrifice acceptable to God is a broken spirit". Promises and vows: a vow is a deliberate and free promise made to God concerning a possible and better good. Social duty of religion and the right to religious freedom: all are bound to seek the truth, especially concerning God and his Church, and to embrace and hold it. The right to religious liberty means that no one is to be forced to act against his convictions, nor restrained from acting in accordance with conscience in religious matters, within due limits.

§ 2110–2117 Superstition is the deviation of religious feeling and practices; it can affect the worship we offer the true God (attributing an importance in some way magical to certain practices, even legitimate ones). Idolatry divinises what is not God: power, pleasure, race, ancestors, the state, money. Divination: all forms are to be rejected: recourse to Satan or demons, conjuring up the dead, horoscopes, astrology, palm reading, interpretation of omens and lots, clairvoyance, mediums. Magic and sorcery, by which one attempts to tame occult powers to have supernatural power over others, are gravely contrary to the virtue of religion. Wearing charms is reprehensible. Spiritism often implies divination or magical practices.

§ 2118–2128 Irreligion: tempting God (putting his goodness and power to the test), sacrilege (profaning sacraments, persons, things or places consecrated to God; especially grave against the Eucharist), and simony (buying or selling spiritual things). Atheism: rejecting or denying God's existence is a sin against the virtue of religion. Believers can have more than a little to do with the rise of atheism, by neglecting their education in the faith, misrepresenting doctrine, or by failings in religious, moral or social life. Agnosticism can sometimes include a search for God, but it can also mean indifferentism or a flight from the ultimate question of existence.

§ 2129–2141 "You shall not make for yourself a graven image." The divine injunction included the prohibition of every representation of God by the hand of man. But God's Incarnation has introduced a new "economy" of images. Basing itself on the Incarnation, the Seventh Ecumenical Council (Nicaea II, 787) justified the veneration of icons of Christ, the Mother of God, the angels and the saints. "The honour rendered to an image passes to its prototype." The veneration of sacred images is a "respectful veneration", not the adoration due to God alone.

Words to know

Idolatry
Giving to a creature the worship due to God alone; includes money, power or pleasure.
Simony
Buying or selling spiritual things.

In the Bible

  • Exod 20:2-5: thou shalt not have strange gods
  • Matt 4:10: the Lord thy God shalt thou adore

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST II-II q.81 a.6: Religion is the virtue by which we give God the worship due to him; it is the greatest moral virtue.
  • ST II-II q.94 a.1: Idolatry is giving divine worship to a creature.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “The honour rendered to an image passes to its prototype.” (St Basil the Great (On the Holy Spirit 18.45))
  • “You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it rests in you.” (St Augustine (Confessions I.1))
  • “Idolatry gives the worship due to God to a creature.” (St Thomas Aquinas (ST II-II q.94 a.1))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Catholics worship statues.
    What the Church teaches: Images receive respectful veneration that passes to the person represented; adoration is due to God alone (§§ 2131–2132).
  • Not quite: Horoscopes and fortune-telling are harmless fun.
    What the Church teaches: All forms of divination are to be rejected because they seek power over time and history apart from God (§ 2116).
  • Not quite: Doubt is always sinful.
    What the Church teaches: Voluntary doubt that refuses revealed truth is a sin; involuntary doubt (difficulty in believing) is different (§ 2088).

To think about

What "other god" competes most with the true God in your life?

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand the first commandment's demands of faith, hope, charity and religion, and the sins of superstition, idolatry, divination, irreligion and atheism.

  1. What are the most common "idols" in South African culture today (money, status, sport, politics, ancestors)?
  2. How would you respond respectfully to a friend who visits a sangoma or consults horoscopes?
  3. How might Christians contribute to atheism, according to § 2125?

Activity: Idol audit: each person writes privately what most competes with God for first place in their time, money and thoughts, then one concrete way to put God first this week.
