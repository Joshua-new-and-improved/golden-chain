The Fourth Commandment

What does the Catholic Church teach about Fourth Commandment? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 2196–2257

In one sentence: Honour your father and mother: the family is the original cell of society; it also covers our duties to authorities and theirs to us.

What the Catechism says

The fourth commandment opens the second table and concerns love of neighbour, beginning with the family. A man and a woman united in marriage, with their children, form a family; it is the original cell of social life and a domestic church.

Children owe their parents respect (filial piety), gratitude, obedience while they live at home, and care in old age. Parents must educate their children in faith and virtue, respect their vocation, and are the first heralds of the faith. The commandment extends to all legitimate authority: citizens should cooperate with the common good, pay taxes, vote and defend the country; authorities must serve the dignity of persons. One must refuse obedience to authorities when their commands are contrary to the moral order.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 2196–2200 "Honour your father and your mother, that your days may be long." The fourth commandment opens the second tablet. It shows us the order of charity: God willed that, after him, we should honour our parents to whom we owe life. It is addressed expressly to children in relation to their parents, but also concerns relationships within the family, with elders, teachers, leaders, magistrates and those who govern. Observing it brings spiritual and temporal fruits of peace and prosperity.

§ 2201–2206 The family in God's plan: a man and a woman united in marriage, together with their children, form a family. This institution is prior to any recognition by public authority, which has an obligation to recognise it. The Christian family is a communion of persons, a sign and image of the communion of the Father and the Son in the Holy Spirit. It is called a domestic church. Family relationships entail particular affinity of feelings, affections and interests, arising above all from the members' respect for one another.

§ 2207–2213 The family and society: the family is the original cell of social life. Authority, stability and a life of relationships within the family constitute the foundations for freedom, security and fraternity within society. The family should live in such a way that its members learn to care and take responsibility for the young, the old, the sick, the handicapped and the poor. Where families cannot do this, other persons, families and society must provide for their needs. The political community has a duty to honour the family, to assist it, and to ensure: freedom to establish a family and raise children according to one's moral and religious convictions; protection of marriage; freedom to profess one's faith and pass it on; the right to private property, free enterprise, work and housing; medical care, assistance for the aged and family benefits; protection of security and health; freedom to form associations.

§ 2214–2220 Duties of children: filial respect (piety) is shown by true docility and obedience. As long as a child lives at home, he should obey his parents in all that they ask of him when it is for his good or that of the family. Grown children should give their parents material and moral support in old age and in times of illness, loneliness or distress. Respect for parents fosters harmony in all family life and among siblings. Christians owe special gratitude to those from whom they received the gift of faith.

§ 2221–2233 Duties of parents: the fecundity of conjugal love is not restricted solely to procreation but extends to children's moral education and spiritual formation. Parents have the first responsibility for the education of their children: creating a home where tenderness, forgiveness, respect, fidelity and disinterested service are the rule; educating in the virtues; giving good example; evangelising them; choosing schools that correspond to their convictions. When children become adults, they have the right and duty to choose their profession and state of life; parents should not exert pressure in choosing a profession or spouse. Family ties are important but not absolute: "He who loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me."

§ 2234–2246 Authorities in civil society: those who exercise authority should do so as a service, respecting fundamental human rights, and making a hierarchy of values that facilitates freedom and responsibility for all. Citizens' duties: to contribute to the good of society in a spirit of truth, justice, solidarity and freedom; to pay taxes, exercise the right to vote, defend one's country; to love and serve one's homeland. The rich nations are obliged to welcome the foreigner in search of security and livelihood, insofar as they are able; immigrants are obliged to respect the material and spiritual heritage of the host country. The citizen is obliged in conscience not to follow the directives of civil authorities when they are contrary to the moral order, the fundamental rights of persons or the teachings of the Gospel: "We must obey God rather than men." Armed resistance to oppression is legitimate only under strict conditions.

Words to know

Filial piety
The respect, gratitude and care children owe their parents.

In the Bible

  • Exod 20:12: honour thy father and thy mother
  • Eph 6:1-4: fathers, provoke not your children
  • Sir 3:1-16: honour thy father

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST II-II q.101 a.1: Piety (pietas) is the virtue of giving honour and service to parents and country, as principles of our being.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “My son, help your father in his old age, and do not grieve him as long as he lives; even if he is lacking in understanding, show forbearance.” (Sirach 3:12–13)
  • “If we would have our children obey us, let us bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord.” (St John Chrysostom (Homily on Ephesians 21))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: The fourth commandment only applies to young children.
    What the Church teaches: It concerns adult children (support in old age), siblings, and all relationships to legitimate authority (§§ 2199, 2218).
  • Not quite: Honouring parents means obeying them in everything, even sin.
    What the Church teaches: Children obey in what is for their good; adults choose their own state of life, and no one may obey commands contrary to the moral law (§§ 2217, 2230, 2242).
  • Not quite: Family ties are absolute.
    What the Church teaches: They are important but subordinate to the call of Christ (§ 2232).

Psychology connection

Honour does not require denying abuse or harm. Attachment theory (Bowlby; Ainsworth) shows how early caregiving shapes later relationships, and therapy for adults with painful family histories often works toward honest acknowledgement, boundaries and, where possible, forgiveness: none of which conflicts with the fourth commandment. Forgiveness interventions show moderate effects on wellbeing (Wade et al. 2014, Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, meta-analysis).
