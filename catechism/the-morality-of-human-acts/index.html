The morality of human acts

What does the Catholic Church teach about morality of human acts? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 1749–1761

In one sentence: An act is good when its object, intention and circumstances are all good; some acts are always wrong, whatever the intention.

What the Catechism says

Freedom makes us moral subjects. The morality of human acts depends on three sources: the object chosen (what is done), the intention (why it is done), and the circumstances (which can increase or reduce goodness or guilt but cannot change the object's moral quality).

A morally good act requires all three to be good. A good intention does not make a bad act good: the end does not justify the means. Some acts, such as blasphemy, murder and adultery, are always gravely wrong by their object, independently of circumstances and intentions. It is wrong to do evil so that good may come of it.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 1749–1750 Freedom makes man a moral subject. When he acts deliberately, man is, so to speak, the father of his acts. Human acts, that is, acts freely chosen in consequence of a judgement of conscience, can be morally evaluated: they are either good or evil. The morality of human acts depends on three "sources": the object chosen; the end in view or the intention; the circumstances of the action.

§ 1751–1754 The object chosen is a good toward which the will deliberately directs itself; it is the matter of a human act, and determines the morality of the act, as reason recognises it as being or not being in conformity with the true good. The intention is on the side of the acting subject: it is a movement of the will toward the end, and a key element in moral evaluation. One action can be inspired by several intentions. A good intention (for example, that of helping one's neighbour) does not make behaviour that is intrinsically disordered, such as lying and calumny, good or just. "The end does not justify the means." A bad intention added to a good act (for example, almsgiving "to be seen by men") makes it evil. Circumstances, including consequences, are secondary elements: they contribute to increasing or diminishing the moral goodness or evil of acts (the amount of a theft), and can diminish or increase responsibility (acting out of fear of death); they cannot of themselves change the moral quality of acts, making good or right an action that is in itself evil.

§ 1755–1761 A morally good act requires the goodness of the object, of the end, and of the circumstances together. An evil end corrupts the action, even if its object is good in itself. The object of the choice can by itself vitiate an act in its entirety: there are some concrete acts, such as fornication, that it is always wrong to choose, because choosing them entails a disorder of the will, a moral evil. It is therefore an error to judge the morality of human acts by considering only the intention that inspires them or the circumstances which supply their context. There are acts which, in and of themselves, independently of circumstances and intentions, are always gravely illicit by reason of their object: blasphemy and perjury, murder and adultery. One may not do evil so that good may result from it.

Words to know

Object
What the acting person chooses to do, as understood by reason.
Intrinsically evil
An act always wrong by its object, whatever the intention or circumstances.

In the Bible

  • Rom 3:8: let us do evil that good may come? God forbid

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST I-II q.18 a.4: An act's goodness comes from its object, end and circumstances; it is good only if all are good ("good from a whole cause, evil from any defect").

From the Fathers and saints

  • “An action is not good simply unless it is good in every respect: bonum ex integra causa, malum ex quocumque defectu (good from a complete cause, evil from any single defect).” (St Thomas Aquinas (ST I-II q.18 a.4 ad 3))
  • “Why not do evil that good may come? Their condemnation is just.” (St Paul (Romans 3:8))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: If my intention is good, the act is good.
    What the Church teaches: A good intention cannot make an intrinsically evil act good: the end does not justify the means (§§ 1753, 1756).
  • Not quite: Morality depends entirely on consequences.
    What the Church teaches: Circumstances and consequences can increase or reduce goodness or evil, but cannot make an evil act good (§ 1754).

To think about

Think of a time you justified a wrong act by a good intention. What would the three sources say?

For catechists

Aim: Participants can apply the three sources of morality (object, intention, circumstances) to real cases.

  1. Apply the three sources to: giving money to charity to impress your boss.
  2. Why can't a good intention justify lying?
  3. What is the difference between an act's object and its consequences?

Activity: Case clinic: give groups three short cases (e.g. telling a lie to protect a friend's feelings; returning lost money; studying hard out of jealousy). Analyse each by object, intention and circumstances.
