The Ninth Commandment

What does the Catholic Church teach about Ninth Commandment? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 2514–2533

In one sentence: Purity of heart: guard your desires, thoughts and gaze, because the pure in heart shall see God.

What the Catechism says

St John distinguishes three kinds of covetousness: lust of the flesh, lust of the eyes and pride of life. The ninth commandment forbids carnal concupiscence; the tenth, coveting another's goods.

The heart is the seat of moral personality. "Blessed are the pure in heart." Purity of heart is won by the virtue and gift of chastity, purity of intention, purity of vision (disciplining feelings and imagination, refusing complicity in impure thoughts) and prayer. Purity requires modesty, which protects the intimate centre of the person and guides how we look at others. Society needs a purification of the social climate, in media and culture.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 2514–2516 St John distinguishes three kinds of covetousness or concupiscence: lust of the flesh, lust of the eyes and pride of life. Following the Catholic catechetical tradition, the ninth commandment forbids carnal concupiscence; the tenth forbids coveting another's goods. Etymologically, "concupiscence" can refer to any intense form of human desire; Christian theology has given it the particular meaning of the movement of the sensitive appetite contrary to the operation of reason. St Paul identifies it with the rebellion of the "flesh" against the "spirit". Concupiscence stems from the disobedience of the first sin; it unsettles man's moral faculties and, without being in itself an offence, inclines man to commit sins. In man, being composed of spirit and body, there already exists a certain tension, a certain struggle of tendencies between "spirit" and "flesh".

§ 2517–2519 Purification of the heart: the heart is the seat of moral personality: "Out of the heart come evil thoughts, murder, adultery, fornication." The struggle against carnal covetousness entails purifying the heart and practising temperance. The "pure in heart" are those who have attuned their intellects and wills to the demands of God's holiness, chiefly in three areas: charity, chastity or sexual rectitude, and love of truth and orthodoxy of faith. "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God." Purity of heart is the precondition of the vision of God; even now it enables us to see according to God, to accept others as "neighbours", and to perceive the human body as a temple of the Holy Spirit.

§ 2520–2527 The battle for purity: Baptism confers purification from all sins, but the baptised must continue to struggle against concupiscence of the flesh and disordered desires. With God's grace he will prevail: by the virtue and gift of chastity, by purity of intention, by purity of vision (discipline of feelings and imagination, refusing complicity in impure thoughts), and by prayer. Purity requires modesty, an integral part of temperance. Modesty protects the intimate centre of the person; it means refusing to unveil what should remain hidden; it is ordered to chastity, guides how one looks at others and behaves toward them, and protects the mystery of persons and their love. Forms of modesty vary from one culture to another, but everywhere it exists as an intuition of the spiritual dignity proper to man. Purity of heart demands a purification of the social climate: of the media, of permissiveness, of a mistaken notion of human freedom.

In the Bible

  • Matt 5:8: blessed are the clean of heart
  • 1 John 2:16: concupiscence of the flesh, of the eyes, pride of life
  • Ps 50:12 (51): create a clean heart in me

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST II-II q.8 a.7: The gift of understanding corresponds to the beatitude of the pure in heart: it purifies the mind to see God.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “The pure in heart are those who have a single, undivided heart: they shall see God.” (St Augustine (Sermon on the Mount I.2.8))
  • “The Lord does not say it is blessed to know something about God, but to have God present within oneself.” (St Gregory of Nyssa (On the Beatitudes 6))
  • “No, it is a sin! God does not want it!” (St Maria Goretti (to her attacker, 1902, as recorded))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Having a sexual thought or temptation is a sin.
    What the Church teaches: Concupiscence and temptation are not in themselves sins; sin lies in consenting, in "complicity" with impure thoughts (§§ 2515, 2520).
  • Not quite: Modesty is only about women's clothing.
    What the Church teaches: Modesty concerns everyone's gaze, speech, behaviour and dress; it protects the mystery of persons (§§ 2521–2524).
  • Not quite: Purity of heart is only about sex.
    What the Church teaches: It covers charity, chastity and love of truth (§ 2518).

Psychology connection

Clinically, intrusive unwanted sexual or blasphemous thoughts are common, and in obsessive-compulsive disorder they become a source of great distress. The Catechism's distinction between a thought arising and consenting to it is pastorally important: trying hard to suppress a thought tends to make it return more (Wegner's "white bear" studies; Wegner et al. 1987, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology). Acceptance-based approaches and ERP help.

To think about

What do you allow into your heart through your eyes (screens, media)? What one change would help?

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand concupiscence, purity of heart in its three areas, the means of the battle for purity, and modesty as respect for the mystery of persons.

  1. What is the difference between a temptation and a sin?
  2. How does purity of heart help us "see according to God" (§ 2519)?
  3. What does modesty look like online (photos, messages, what we view)?

Activity: Custody of the eyes and heart: discuss practical ways to guard what enters through screens and imagination, and pray together the Beatitude "Blessed are the pure in heart" as a short daily prayer.
