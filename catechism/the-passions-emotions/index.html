The passions (emotions)

What does the Catholic Church teach about passions (emotions)? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 1762–1775

In one sentence: Emotions are part of being human; in themselves neither good nor bad, they become morally good when reason and will direct them to the good.

What the Catechism says

The passions, or emotions, are movements of feeling that incline us to act or not act in regard to something felt or imagined to be good or evil. Love, aroused by the attraction of the good, is the most fundamental; others include desire and fear, joy and sadness, and anger.

In themselves the passions are neither good nor evil. They are morally qualified only insofar as they depend on reason and will. Moral perfection is not to suppress them but to integrate them: the more they are directed by right reason to the good, the more they contribute to a good act. Strong feelings are not decisive for the morality or holiness of a person; they are a reservoir of images and affections in which the moral life is expressed.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 1762–1766 The human person is ordered to beatitude by his deliberate acts; the passions or feelings he experiences can dispose him to it and contribute to it. "Passions" means emotions or movements of the sensitive appetite that incline us to act or not to act in regard to something felt or imagined to be good or evil. They form the passageway and ensure the connection between the life of the senses and the life of the mind. The most fundamental passion is love, aroused by the attraction of the good. Love causes a desire for the absent good and the hope of obtaining it; this movement finds completion in the pleasure and joy of the good possessed. The apprehension of evil causes hatred, aversion and fear of the impending evil; this movement ends in sadness at some present evil, or in the anger that resists it. "To love is to will the good of another" (Aquinas).

§ 1767–1770 In themselves passions are neither good nor evil. They are morally qualified only to the extent that they effectively engage reason and will. Passions are said to be voluntary "either because they are commanded by the will or because the will does not place obstacles in their way". It belongs to the perfection of the moral or human good that the passions be governed by reason. Strong feelings are not decisive for the morality or holiness of persons; they are simply the inexhaustible reservoir of images and affections in which the moral life is expressed. Emotions and feelings can be taken up into the virtues or perverted by the vices. In the Christian life the Holy Spirit accomplishes his work by mobilising the whole being, with all its sorrows, fears and sadness, as is visible in the Lord's agony and passion. Moral perfection consists in man's being moved to the good not by his will alone, but also by his "heart".

Words to know

Passion
In Thomas's sense, an emotion: a movement of the sensory appetite in response to good or evil.

In the Bible

  • Mark 14:33-36: he began to fear and to be heavy
  • Eph 4:26: be angry, and sin not

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST I-II qq.22–48: Thomas's treatise on the passions names eleven basic emotions: love, hatred, desire, aversion, joy, sorrow, hope, despair, fear, daring and anger.
  • ST I-II q.24 a.3: Passions that follow and are guided by reason increase the goodness of an act.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “To love is to will the good of someone.” (St Thomas Aquinas (ST I-II q.26 a.4))
  • “If the will is perverse, the movements will be perverse; if it is right, they will be not only blameless but praiseworthy.” (St Augustine (City of God XIV.6))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Feeling angry (or afraid, or attracted) is a sin.
    What the Church teaches: Passions in themselves are neither good nor evil; they become moral only as they engage reason and will (§ 1767).
  • Not quite: Holiness means having no strong emotions.
    What the Church teaches: Strong feelings are not decisive for holiness; the goal is for the heart to be moved to the good too (§§ 1768–1770). Christ himself wept and was troubled.
  • Not quite: Emotions should simply be followed.
    What the Church teaches: They are to be governed by reason and taken up into the virtues (§§ 1767–1768).

Psychology connection

Thomas's view anticipates modern emotion science: appraisal theories see emotions as evaluations of something as good or bad for us, and emotion-regulation research (Gross, 1998) describes reshaping emotions through attention and reappraisal, close to Thomas's "political" rule of reason over the passions, rather than "despotic" suppression.

Aquinas's view that emotions are neither good nor bad in themselves, but are to be shaped by reason, closely parallels emotion-regulation research (Gross 1998, 2015): strategies like cognitive reappraisal are generally linked to better outcomes than suppression. CBT and DBT both teach that feelings are valid signals to be understood, not commands to be obeyed or enemies to be crushed.

To think about

What emotion was strongest in you today? What good was it pointing to?

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand the passions in Thomistic terms, their moral neutrality, and their integration into virtue.

  1. Name the eleven passions in Aquinas's scheme (love, hatred, desire, aversion, joy, sadness; hope, despair, fear, daring, anger). Which do you notice most?
  2. When is anger good? When is it sinful?
  3. What does it mean to be moved to the good "by the heart"?

Activity: Emotion journal: for three days, note one strong feeling each day, what good or evil it was responding to, and what reason said about how to act on it.
