The resurrection of the body

What does the Catholic Church teach about resurrection of the body? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 988–1019

In one sentence: Our bodies will rise again, transformed, as Christ's did; so we must honour the body and prepare for a holy death.

What the Catechism says

We believe not only that the soul lives on, but that our mortal bodies will come to life again. This was hard to accept from the beginning, yet it is central: if there is no resurrection, Christ is not risen. All the dead will rise at the last day: those who have done good to the resurrection of life, those who have done evil to the resurrection of judgement.

How? We cannot imagine it; but it will be our own body, glorified. Death is the end of earthly life and the consequence of sin, but Christ has transformed it: for the Christian, dying is going home to the Lord. The Church encourages us to prepare for the hour of death.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 988–991 The Creed culminates in the proclamation of the resurrection of the dead on the last day and in life everlasting. We firmly believe and hope that, just as Christ is truly risen and lives for ever, so after death the righteous will live for ever with the risen Christ and he will raise them up on the last day. Belief in the resurrection of the dead has been an essential element of Christian faith from its beginnings: "How can some of you say that there is no resurrection of the dead?"

§ 992–996 God revealed the resurrection of the dead to his people progressively: hope in the bodily resurrection grew from faith in the Creator of the whole person, soul and body, and in his faithfulness to his covenant (the martyrs of 2 Maccabees 7; Daniel 12). The Pharisees and many of Jesus' contemporaries hoped for it. Jesus teaches it firmly, links it to faith in himself ("I am the resurrection and the life"), and gives signs by raising the dead. From the beginning Christian faith in the resurrection met incomprehension and opposition: "On no point does the Christian faith encounter more opposition than on the resurrection of the body" (St Augustine).

§ 997–1001 What is "rising"? In death the soul is separated from the body, which decays, while the soul goes to meet God, waiting to be reunited with its glorified body. God, in his almighty power, will definitively grant incorruptible life to our bodies by reuniting them with our souls through Jesus' Resurrection. Who will rise? All the dead: "those who have done good, to the resurrection of life, and those who have done evil, to the resurrection of judgement". How? Christ rose with his own body; we too shall rise with our own bodies, but transformed into a glorious, spiritual body. This "how" exceeds our understanding; it is accessible only to faith. Our participation in the Eucharist already gives us a foretaste. When? Definitively at the last day, at the end of the world.

§ 1002–1004 Risen with Christ: in a certain way we have already risen with Christ through Baptism, and our Christian life now shares in his risen life, hidden with Christ in God. Our bodies, members of Christ and temples of the Spirit, must be respected: this teaching inspires respect for one's own body and for others', especially the suffering.

§ 1005–1014 Dying in Christ Jesus: to rise with Christ, we must die with Christ. Death is the end of earthly life; it is a consequence of sin, but Christ transformed it: through Baptism the Christian has already sacramentally "died with Christ", and physical death completes this, "going home to the Lord". Death puts an end to our time of grace and mercy: "it is appointed for men to die once". There is no reincarnation after death. The Church encourages us to prepare for death, asking the Mother of God to pray for us "now and at the hour of our death", and to entrust ourselves to St Joseph, patron of a happy death.

Words to know

Resurrection of the flesh
The raising of our bodies on the last day, reunited with our souls.

In the Bible

  • John 11:25: I am the resurrection
  • 1 Cor 15:35-44: it is sown a natural body
  • Phil 3:20-21: he will reform the body of our lowness

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • Supplement q.79 a.1 (from Thomas's Sentences commentary): We will rise with the same body, because the soul is naturally the form of a body; a soul without its body is not a complete human being.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “On no point does the Christian faith encounter more opposition than on the resurrection of the body.” (St Augustine (Enarrationes in Psalmos 88.2.5))
  • “Praised be you, my Lord, through Sister Death, from whom no living person can escape.” (St Francis of Assisi (Canticle of the Creatures))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Christians believe only that the soul survives death.
    What the Church teaches: We believe in the resurrection of the body as well: our own bodies, glorified (§§ 990, 999).
  • Not quite: Reincarnation is compatible with Christian faith.
    What the Church teaches: There is no reincarnation after death; we die once (§ 1013).
  • Not quite: The body is unimportant, since only the soul is saved.
    What the Church teaches: The body is a temple of the Spirit and will rise; it deserves respect (§ 1004).

To think about

Pray for a holy death today: yours and that of someone who is dying.

For catechists

Aim: Participants can explain the resurrection of the body, the Christian meaning of death, and its implications for how we treat the body.

  1. Why do you think the resurrection of the body meets so much opposition (Augustine)?
  2. How does the Eucharist give us a foretaste of the resurrection (§ 1000)?
  3. How does belief in the resurrection change how we treat our bodies and the sick?

Activity: Read 1 Corinthians 15:35–44 and look at a seed beside a plant or flower. Discuss the image Paul uses: continuity and transformation.
