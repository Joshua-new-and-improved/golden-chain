The Ten Commandments

What does the Catholic Church teach about Ten Commandments? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 2052–2082

In one sentence: The commandments are God's word to his people, a path of life, summed up by Jesus in love of God and neighbour.

What the Catechism says

"Teacher, what must I do?" Jesus answers: keep the commandments, and follow me. The Ten Commandments (Decalogue, "ten words") were given by God on Sinai within the covenant: they express what it means to belong to him. They are a privileged expression of the natural law.

Jesus summed them up in two: love God with all your heart, and your neighbour as yourself. The first three concern love of God, the other seven love of neighbour. They form an organic unity: to break one is to offend against all. They bind gravely, and what God commands he makes possible by his grace.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 2052–2055 "Teacher, what good deed must I do, to have eternal life?" Jesus answers: "If you would enter life, keep the commandments", and then, "If you would be perfect, go, sell what you possess… and come, follow me." The commandments are the first step on the way to freedom; following Christ fulfils them. Jesus summed them up in the twofold commandment of love: "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart… and your neighbour as yourself. On these two commandments depend all the law and the prophets." The Decalogue must be interpreted in the light of this twofold and yet single commandment of love, the fullness of the Law.

§ 2056–2063 "Decalogue" means literally "ten words". God revealed them to his people on the holy mountain, written "with the finger of God". They are found in Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5. The Decalogue is understood first in the context of the Exodus, God's great liberating event: "I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage." The commandments express the implications of belonging to God through the covenant. Moral existence is a response to the Lord's loving initiative.

§ 2064–2068 In fidelity to Scripture and Jesus' example, the Tradition of the Church has acknowledged the primordial importance and significance of the Decalogue. Since St Augustine, the Ten Commandments have occupied a predominant place in catechesis. The division and numbering have varied: the present catechism follows Augustine's division, traditional in the Catholic Church and also among Lutherans; the Greek Fathers worked out a slightly different division, found in the Orthodox Churches and Reformed communities. The Council of Trent teaches that the Ten Commandments are obligatory for Christians and that the justified man is still bound to keep them.

§ 2069–2082 The Decalogue forms a coherent whole: each "word" refers to each of the others and to all of them; to transgress one commandment is to infringe all the others. The first three concern love of God, the other seven love of neighbour. The Decalogue contains a privileged expression of the natural law. The commandments are fundamentally immutable and oblige always and everywhere; their obligations are grave in themselves, though obedience can involve light matter (an abusive word is forbidden by the fifth, but would be a grave offence only as a result of circumstances or intention). What God commands he makes possible by his grace: "Apart from me you can do nothing."

Words to know

Decalogue
The Ten Commandments; literally "ten words".

In the Bible

  • Exod 20:1-17: the Ten Commandments
  • Matt 19:16-21: if thou wilt enter into life
  • Matt 22:37-40: on these two commandments

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST I-II q.100 a.3: All the moral precepts reduce to the Ten Commandments, as conclusions to principles.
  • ST I-II q.100 a.8: The precepts of the Decalogue admit no dispensation.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “The ten commandments are written on two tablets… the first three concern God, the seven others the neighbour.” (St Augustine (Sermon 33.2.2))
  • “From the beginning, God had implanted in the heart of man the precepts of the natural law. Then he was content to remind him of them. This was the Decalogue.” (St Irenaeus (Against Heresies IV.15.1))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Protestants and Catholics have different commandments.
    What the Church teaches: The content is the same Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5 text; only the numbering differs (Augustine's division vs. the Greek Fathers') (§ 2066).
  • Not quite: Christians are no longer bound by the Ten Commandments.
    What the Church teaches: Trent and the whole Tradition teach that the justified are still bound by them; Christ fulfils them (§ 2068).
  • Not quite: The commandments are mainly prohibitions.
    What the Church teaches: They are the response to God's liberating love, interpreted by the twofold commandment of love (§§ 2055, 2061–2062).

To think about

Pray through the Ten Commandments as an examination of conscience tonight.

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand the Decalogue as covenant response, its unity, its numbering, and its link to the law of love.

  1. Why does the Decalogue begin by recalling the Exodus ("I brought you out…")?
  2. What is the difference between keeping the commandments and following Christ (Matt 19:16–21)?
  3. Why can't you break just one commandment?

Activity: Compare Exodus 20:1–17 with Deuteronomy 5:6–21 and note differences (such as the reason for the Sabbath). Then write the commandments in Catholic and Reformed numbering side by side.
