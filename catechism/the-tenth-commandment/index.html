The Tenth Commandment

What does the Catholic Church teach about Tenth Commandment? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 2534–2557

In one sentence: Do not covet: avoid greed and envy, seek poverty of heart, and desire God above all.

What the Catechism says

The tenth commandment concerns the intentions of the heart: it forbids greed, avarice and the desire to acquire earthly goods without limit, and envy, sadness at the sight of another's goods. Envy is a capital sin and can lead to the gravest crimes; it is overcome by goodwill, humility and rejoicing in others' progress.

The Holy Spirit turns our desires to the good. Jesus calls his disciples to prefer him above everything and everyone, to poverty of heart: detachment from riches. "Blessed are the poor in spirit." The deepest desire of the human heart is expressed in the words: "I want to see God."

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 2534–2537 "You shall not covet your neighbour's house… or anything that is your neighbour's." The tenth commandment unfolds and completes the ninth, which is concerned with concupiscence of the flesh. It forbids coveting the goods of another, as the root of theft, robbery and fraud, which the seventh forbids. "Lust of the eyes" leads to the violence and injustice forbidden by the fifth. Cupidity has its origin, like fornication, in the idolatry prohibited by the first three commandments. The tenth commandment concerns the intentions of the heart; with the ninth, it summarises all the precepts of the Law. The sensitive appetite leads us to desire pleasant things we do not have; these desires are good in themselves, but often exceed the limits of reason and drive us to covet unjustly what belongs to another.

§ 2538–2540 Envy: the tenth commandment requires that envy be banished from the human heart. Envy can lead to the worst crimes: "through the devil's envy death entered the world". Envy is a capital sin: it refers to the sadness at the sight of another's goods and the immoderate desire to acquire them for oneself, even unjustly. When it wishes grave harm to a neighbour, it is a mortal sin. St Augustine saw envy as "the diabolical sin": from it are born hatred, detraction, calumny, joy caused by the misfortune of a neighbour, and displeasure caused by his prosperity. Envy represents a form of sadness and therefore a refusal of charity. The baptised person should struggle against it by exercising good-will. Envy often comes from pride; the baptised person should train himself to live in humility. "Would you like to see God glorified by you? Then rejoice in your brother's progress and you will immediately give glory to God" (Chrysostom).

§ 2541–2550 The desires of the Spirit: the economy of law and grace turns men's hearts away from avarice and envy; it initiates them into desire for the Sovereign Good. Christ's faithful have "crucified the flesh with its passions and desires". Poverty of heart: Jesus enjoins his disciples to prefer him to everything and everyone, and bids them "renounce all that they have". "Blessed are the poor in spirit": abandonment to the providence of the Father in heaven frees us from anxiety about tomorrow. Trust in God is a preparation for the blessedness of the poor: they shall see God. "I want to see God": desire for true happiness frees man from his immoderate attachment to the goods of this world so that he can find his fulfilment in the vision and beatitude of God. "There we shall rest and see, we shall see and love, we shall love and praise. Behold what will be at the end without end" (Augustine, City of God XXII.30).

Words to know

Envy
Sadness at another's good and the desire to have it, even unjustly.
Poverty of heart
Detachment from riches, so that God is our treasure.

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: The tenth commandment only matters if I act on my desire.
    What the Church teaches: It concerns the intentions of the heart themselves; envy and covetousness are sins of the heart (§§ 2534–2535).
  • Not quite: Wanting nice things is sinful.
    What the Church teaches: Desires for pleasant things are good in themselves; the sin is coveting unjustly what belongs to another (§ 2535).

Psychology connection

Social comparison (Festinger, 1954) is a basic human tendency, and constant upward comparison, for example on social media, is associated with lower wellbeing. The tenth commandment's remedies, gratitude and rejoicing in others' good, have parallels in research on gratitude practices.

Gratitude interventions, like the gratitude journal suggested here, show small but reliable improvements in wellbeing in meta-analyses (Davis et al. 2016, Journal of Counseling Psychology). Research on social-media use links passive scrolling and upward social comparison to envy and lower wellbeing (Verduyn et al. 2017, Social Issues and Policy Review), giving a modern face to the tenth commandment.

To think about

Whose success do you find hard to celebrate? Pray for them by name.

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand covetousness and envy, poverty of heart, and how desire for God frees us from disordered desire.

  1. How does social media feed envy and covetousness?
  2. Why does Chrysostom say rejoicing in another's progress glorifies God?
  3. What would "poverty of heart" look like in your spending, studies or career plans?

Activity: Gratitude and rejoicing practice: for a week, write down three things you are grateful for each day, and one person whose good fortune you will deliberately rejoice in and pray for.
