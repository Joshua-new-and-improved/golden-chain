The virtues

What does the Catholic Church teach about virtues? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 1803–1845

In one sentence: Virtues are firm habits of doing good: four cardinal (human) virtues, three theological virtues, and the Spirit's gifts and fruits.

What the Catechism says

A virtue is a habitual and firm disposition to do the good. Human virtues are acquired by education, deliberate acts and perseverance, and purified by grace. Four play a pivotal ("cardinal") role: prudence (right reason in action), justice (giving God and neighbour their due), fortitude (firmness in difficulties) and temperance (moderating the attraction of pleasures).

The theological virtues, faith, hope and charity, are infused by God and relate directly to him; charity is the greatest, the form of all the virtues. The seven gifts of the Holy Spirit make us docile to his promptings, and his fruits (charity, joy, peace, patience…) are the first-fruits of eternal glory.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 1803–1804 "Whatever is true, whatever is honourable… think about these things" (Phil 4:8). A virtue is a habitual and firm disposition to do the good. It allows the person not only to perform good acts, but to give the best of himself. "The goal of a virtuous life is to become like God" (Gregory of Nyssa). Human virtues are firm attitudes, stable dispositions, habitual perfections of intellect and will that govern our actions, order our passions and guide our conduct according to reason and faith. They make possible ease, self-mastery and joy in leading a morally good life.

§ 1805–1809 The cardinal virtues: four virtues play a pivotal role (cardo = hinge). Prudence disposes practical reason to discern our true good in every circumstance and to choose the right means of achieving it; it is "right reason in action" and the "charioteer of the virtues", guiding the others. Justice is the constant and firm will to give God and neighbour their due (towards God, the "virtue of religion"). Fortitude ensures firmness in difficulties and constancy in the pursuit of the good; it strengthens resolve to resist temptations and overcome obstacles, and enables one to conquer fear, even fear of death. Temperance moderates the attraction of pleasures and provides balance in the use of created goods; it ensures the will's mastery over instincts and keeps desires within the limits of what is honourable.

§ 1810–1811 The human virtues are acquired by education, by deliberate acts, and by a perseverance ever renewed in repeated efforts; they are purified and elevated by divine grace. It is not easy for man, wounded by sin, to maintain moral balance. Christ's gift of salvation offers us the grace necessary to persevere in the pursuit of the virtues.

§ 1812–1829 The theological virtues relate directly to God: they have the One and Triune God for their origin, motive and object. They are infused by God into the souls of the faithful to make them capable of acting as his children and of meriting eternal life; they are the foundation of Christian moral activity and animate the human virtues. Faith: by which we believe in God and all he has revealed, and that the Church proposes; "faith apart from works is dead". Hope: by which we desire the Kingdom of heaven and eternal life as our happiness, placing our trust in Christ's promises and relying on the grace of the Spirit; it keeps man from discouragement and sustains him. Charity: by which we love God above all things for his own sake, and our neighbour as ourselves for the love of God; it is the "bond of perfection" and the form of all the virtues.

§ 1830–1845 The gifts and fruits of the Holy Spirit: the moral life of Christians is sustained by the gifts of the Spirit, permanent dispositions that make man docile in following his promptings. The seven gifts are wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety and fear of the Lord. The fruits are perfections the Spirit forms in us as the first fruits of eternal glory; tradition lists twelve: charity, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, generosity, gentleness, faithfulness, modesty, self-control, chastity.

Words to know

Cardinal virtues
Prudence, justice, fortitude and temperance: the "hinges" (cardo) of the moral life.
Theological virtues
Faith, hope and charity: given by God, directed to God.

In the Bible

  • Wis 8:7: she teacheth temperance, prudence, justice and fortitude
  • 1 Cor 13:13: faith, hope, charity; the greatest is charity
  • Gal 5:22-23: the fruit of the Spirit

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST I-II q.61 a.2: Why there are exactly four cardinal virtues: reason itself (prudence), operations (justice), and the passions to be restrained (temperance) or strengthened (fortitude).
  • ST I-II q.68 a.1: The gifts of the Spirit dispose us to follow his promptings readily.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “The goal of a virtuous life is to become like God.” (St Gregory of Nyssa (On the Beatitudes 1))
  • “To live well is nothing other than to love God with all one's heart, with all one's soul and with all one's efforts.” (St Augustine (On the Morals of the Catholic Church 25.46))
  • “Charity is the form of all the virtues.” (St Thomas Aquinas (ST II-II q.23 a.8))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Virtue means being prim or never having fun.
    What the Church teaches: Virtue makes possible ease, self-mastery and joy in doing good (§ 1804).
  • Not quite: We can become virtuous by willpower alone.
    What the Church teaches: Human virtues are acquired by repeated effort but need grace to be purified and elevated; the theological virtues are infused (§§ 1810–1813).
  • Not quite: Prudence means being cautious.
    What the Church teaches: Prudence is right reason in action: seeing the true good and choosing the right means (§ 1806).

Psychology connection

Virtue is built like habits are built: by repeated acts in a stable context. Lally et al. (2010, European Journal of Social Psychology, N = 96) found new habits took a median of about 66 days to become automatic, with wide variation; missing a single day did not undo progress. Thomas would agree that virtue grows by acts, and that grace heals and raises it.

Habit-formation research supports the Thomistic claim that virtues are acquired by repetition: in a naturalistic study, new behaviours took a median of about 66 days to become automatic, with wide variation (Lally et al. 2010, European Journal of Social Psychology, N=96). Peterson and Seligman's character-strengths project (2004) explicitly drew on the classical virtues, including the cardinal ones.

To think about

Which cardinal virtue is weakest in you? Choose one small daily act to train it.

For catechists

Aim: Participants can name and define the cardinal and theological virtues, gifts and fruits, and plan to grow in one virtue.

  1. Which cardinal virtue is your strongest? Your weakest?
  2. Why is prudence called the "charioteer" of the virtues?
  3. How do faith, hope and charity change the human virtues?

Activity: Virtue plan: choose one virtue and one small daily act that exercises it (e.g. fortitude: do the hardest task first). Track it for 30 days, like the site's challenges.
