The Word became flesh

What does the Catholic Church teach about Word became flesh? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 456–483

In one sentence: The eternal Son became truly man without ceasing to be truly God: one Person in two natures.

What the Catechism says

Why did the Word become flesh? To save us by reconciling us with God; so that we might know God's love; to be our model of holiness; and to make us "partakers of the divine nature".

The Church spent centuries defending this mystery against errors that denied either his divinity or his humanity. The councils (Nicaea, Ephesus, Chalcedon, Constantinople) taught that Jesus is one divine Person with two natures, divine and human, without confusion or separation. He has a true human soul, with human knowledge and a human will, freely in harmony with his divine will; and a human heart that loves us.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 456–460 Why did the Word become flesh? With the Nicene Creed we answer: "For us men and for our salvation." (1) To save us by reconciling us with God. (2) So that we might know God's love. (3) To be our model of holiness. (4) To make us "partakers of the divine nature": "the Son of God became man so that we might become God" (St Athanasius), sharing his life by grace.

§ 461–463 Taking up St John's expression "the Word became flesh", the Church calls "Incarnation" the fact that the Son of God assumed a human nature to accomplish our salvation in it. Belief in the true Incarnation is the distinctive sign of Christian faith: "Every spirit which confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is of God."

§ 464–469 The Incarnation does not mean that Jesus is part God and part man, nor a confused mixture. He became truly man while remaining truly God. The Church had to defend this against heresies: Gnostic Docetism (denying his true humanity); Arianism (denying his divinity; Nicaea 325); Nestorianism (making Christ two persons; Ephesus 431 called Mary Theotokos, Mother of God); Monophysitism (merging the natures; Chalcedon 451: one Person "in two natures, without confusion, change, division or separation"). Constantinople II (553) confessed that "one of the Trinity" was crucified. Christ is "one of the Trinity".

§ 470–474 Because the human nature was assumed, not absorbed, the Church has confessed the full reality of Christ's human soul, with its operations of intellect and will, and of his human body. Everything in his humanity is attributed to his divine Person. His human soul had true human knowledge, which could "increase in wisdom"; at the same time it knew intimately the Father and the eternal plans it came to reveal.

§ 475–478 Christ has two wills and two natural operations, divine and human, not opposed but cooperating, so that the human will freely follows his divine will (Constantinople III, 681). Christ's body was truly human: he can be portrayed (Nicaea II, 787, defended icons). Jesus knew and loved us each and all during his life, his agony and his Passion, and gave himself up for each one of us: "The Son of God loved me and gave himself for me." His Sacred Heart is the sign and symbol of that love.

Words to know

Incarnation
The Son of God taking a human nature to accomplish our salvation.
Hypostatic union
The union of two natures, divine and human, in the one Person of the Son.

In the Bible

  • John 1:14: the Word was made flesh
  • 2 Pet 1:4: partakers of the divine nature
  • Phil 2:6-7: emptied himself

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST III q.1 a.2: The Incarnation was the most fitting way to heal us: it strengthens faith, raises hope, enkindles charity and shows the dignity of human nature.
  • ST III q.2 a.2: The union of the Word and humanity took place in the Person, not in the nature.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “The Son of God became man so that we might become God.” (St Athanasius (On the Incarnation 54))
  • “What has not been assumed has not been healed.” (St Gregory of Nazianzus (Letter 101))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Jesus is half God and half man.
    What the Church teaches: He is fully God and fully man, one divine Person in two natures (§ 464).
  • Not quite: Jesus only seemed to be human (or only seemed to suffer).
    What the Church teaches: This is Docetism; his humanity, body and soul, is fully real (§§ 465, 470).
  • Not quite: Jesus did not have a real human mind or will.
    What the Church teaches: He has a true human intellect and will, freely united to the divine will (§§ 472, 475).

To think about

Jesus has a human heart that knows and loves you personally (CCC 478). How does that change how you pray?

For catechists

Aim: Participants can state the doctrine of the Incarnation precisely, name the main heresies, and see why it matters for salvation.

  1. Why does it matter that Jesus is fully God? Fully human?
  2. What does Athanasius mean by "so that we might become God"?
  3. How does knowing Jesus loved you personally in his Passion (§ 478) change your prayer?

Activity: Heresy detective: give groups short statements ("Jesus only appeared to be human", "Jesus was a created being", "Mary is only the mother of Jesus' humanity") and ask them to name the error and the council that answered it.
