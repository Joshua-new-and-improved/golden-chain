Vocal prayer, meditation and contemplation

What does the Catholic Church teach about vocal prayer, meditation and contemplation? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 2697–2724

In one sentence: Three expressions of prayer: vocal prayer with words, meditation that seeks with the mind, and contemplation, a gaze of faith fixed on Jesus.

What the Catechism says

We need to pray at specific times so as to learn to pray at all times. Christian tradition has three major expressions of prayer. Vocal prayer, with words spoken or sung, unites body and soul; Jesus taught one, the Our Father. Meditation is a quest: the mind seeks to understand the why and how of the Christian life, often with Scripture, icons or spiritual writers, to move to the conversion of the heart.

Contemplative prayer is "nothing else than a close sharing between friends" (St Teresa of Avila): a gaze of faith fixed on Jesus, a silent love. It is a gift, received in humility and poverty. "I look at him and he looks at me," said a peasant to St John Vianney.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 2697–2699 Prayer is the life of the new heart. It ought to animate us at every moment. But we tend to forget him who is our life and our all. "We must remember God more often than we draw breath" (Gregory of Nazianzus). We cannot pray "at all times" if we do not pray at specific times, consciously willing it. The Church proposes certain rhythms of praying intended to nourish continual prayer: morning and evening prayer, grace before and after meals, the Liturgy of the Hours; Sundays centred on the Eucharist; the cycle of the liturgical year. The Christian tradition has retained three major expressions of prayer: vocal, meditative and contemplative. One thing is common to them: composure of heart.

§ 2700–2704 Vocal prayer: through his Word God speaks to man; by words, mental or vocal, our prayer takes flesh. Most important is that the heart be present to him to whom we are speaking. Jesus prayed vocally (the Our Father, his great priestly prayer). Vocal prayer is an essential element of the Christian life; it associates the body with interior prayer, and corresponds to our human nature. It is the prayer par excellence of crowds, and it can become an initial form of contemplative prayer.

§ 2705–2708 Meditation is above all a quest. The mind seeks to understand the why and how of the Christian life, in order to adhere and respond to what the Lord is asking. Helps: Scripture (especially the Gospels), holy icons, liturgical texts, the writings of the spiritual fathers, works of spirituality, the great book of creation and of history. To meditate on what we read helps us to make it our own by confronting it with ourselves. Methods of meditation are as diverse as spiritual masters; a method is only a guide, the important thing is to advance, with the Holy Spirit, along the one way of prayer: Christ Jesus. Meditation engages thought, imagination, emotion and desire, to deepen our convictions of faith, prompt conversion of heart, and strengthen our will to follow Christ. Christian prayer tries above all to meditate on the mysteries of Christ, as in lectio divina or the Rosary.

§ 2709–2724 Contemplative prayer: "Contemplative prayer is nothing else than a close sharing between friends; it means taking time frequently to be alone with him who we know loves us" (Teresa of Avila). Contemplative prayer seeks him "whom my soul loves". One does not undertake contemplative prayer only when one has the time: one makes time for the Lord, with the firm determination not to give up, no matter what trials and dryness one may encounter. Entering into it is like entering into the Eucharistic liturgy: we gather up the heart, recollect our whole being under the prompting of the Spirit. It is a gaze of faith fixed on Jesus: "I look at him and he looks at me" (the peasant of Ars, to St John Vianney). It is hearing the Word of God, silence, the "symbol of the world to come". It is a union with the prayer of Christ insofar as it makes us participate in his mystery; it is a communion of love bearing life for the multitude, a covenant relationship established by God within our hearts.

Words to know

Meditation
Prayer that engages thought, imagination, emotion and desire to reflect on God's word.
Contemplation
A loving gaze of faith on God; a gift of silent communion.

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST II-II q.180 a.4: Contemplation is principally the contemplation of divine truth, the goal of all human life.

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Vocal prayer is for beginners; real Christians only meditate.
    What the Church teaches: Vocal prayer is essential; Jesus prayed vocally, and it can become contemplative (§§ 2700–2704).
  • Not quite: Christian meditation is the same as emptying the mind.
    What the Church teaches: Christian meditation engages thought, imagination and desire on the mysteries of Christ; contemplation is a loving gaze on a Person (§§ 2708, 2715).
  • Not quite: Dryness in prayer means you're doing it wrong.
    What the Church teaches: One makes time for the Lord whatever trials and dryness one encounters (§ 2710).

Psychology connection

Christian meditation is not the same as secular mindfulness: it is directed to a Person, not only attention to the present. Still, both train attention, and attention-training is linked with better emotion regulation; Christian contemplation adds the relationship that gives silence its meaning.

Mindfulness-based interventions show moderate effects on anxiety, depression and stress (Goldberg et al. 2018, Clinical Psychology Review, meta-analysis). Christian contemplation shares practices with mindfulness (attention, stillness, non-judgemental awareness) but differs in its object: not awareness for its own sake but a relationship with a Person. Christian-accommodated versions of these programmes have also been developed and studied.

To think about

Try five minutes of silent prayer today: just look at Jesus (perhaps before a crucifix) and let him look at you.

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand and practise the three expressions of prayer: vocal, meditation and contemplation, and commit to a daily rhythm.

  1. What daily rhythm of prayer would be realistic for you (morning offering, grace, Angelus, night examen)?
  2. How is Christian meditation different from mindfulness or Eastern meditation?
  3. What does "I look at him and he looks at me" teach about contemplation?

Activity: Ten minutes of silent prayer before the Blessed Sacrament (or a crucifix): begin with a short vocal prayer, read a few verses of a Gospel, then simply remain with Jesus.
