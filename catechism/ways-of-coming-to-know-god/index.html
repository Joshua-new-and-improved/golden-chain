Ways of coming to know God

What does the Catholic Church teach about ways of coming to know God? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 31–35

In one sentence: Both the world and the human person point to God, with real and reasonable certainty.

What the Catechism says

Made in God's image, we can find "ways" to God. These are not proofs in the sense of the natural sciences, but converging and convincing arguments that give real certainty.

There are two starting points. The first is the world: change, contingency, order and beauty point to a first cause. The second is the human person: our openness to truth and beauty, our sense of moral goodness, freedom and conscience, our longing for the infinite, and a spiritual soul that cannot be explained by matter alone.

Neither the world nor we are our own first principle. Reason can know that God exists as a personal God, but to enter his intimate life we need him to reveal himself. The arguments dispose us to faith.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 31 Created in God's image, the person who seeks God discovers certain "ways" of coming to know him. These are not proofs in the sense of the natural sciences, but "converging and convincing arguments" that give real certainty about the truth.

§ 32 The world: from motion and becoming, contingency, and the world's order and beauty, one can come to know God as the origin and end of the universe (Rom 1:19–20). St Augustine: question the beauty of the earth, the sea, the sky; they answer, "See, we are beautiful."

§ 33 The human person: our openness to truth and beauty, our sense of moral goodness, freedom and the voice of conscience, our longing for the infinite and for happiness, all point to a spiritual soul, which cannot be explained by matter alone. The soul, "the seed of eternity we bear in ourselves", can have its origin only in God.

§ 34 The world and the human person attest that they contain within themselves neither their first principle nor their final end; they participate in Being itself, which alone is without origin or end. So, in different ways, we can come to know God, the first cause and final end of all things.

§ 35 Our faculties make us capable of knowing the existence of a personal God. But for us to enter into real intimacy with him, God willed to reveal himself and give us the grace to welcome that revelation in faith. The proofs of God's existence can predispose us to faith and help show that faith is not opposed to reason.

Words to know

Converging arguments
Several independent lines of reasoning pointing to the same conclusion.
Contingency
Existing, but not having to exist: everything we see could have not been.

In the Bible

  • Rom 1:19-20: his invisible things are seen through what is made
  • Wis 13:1-5: from the beauty of creatures

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST I q.2 a.3: Thomas's Five Ways: from motion, causation, contingency, degrees of perfection and the order of nature, to God.
  • ST I q.2 a.2: God's existence can be demonstrated, because we can reason from effects to their cause.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “Question the beauty of the earth, the sea, the sky… they all answer: "See, we are beautiful." Who made them, if not the Beautiful One?” (St Augustine (Sermon 241))
  • “The Five Ways: motion, causation, contingency, degrees of perfection, and order in nature.” (St Thomas Aquinas (ST I q.2 a.3))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Belief in God is blind and irrational.
    What the Church teaches: Reason can reach real certainty that God exists through converging arguments (§§ 31, 35).
  • Not quite: If God can be proved, we don't need faith.
    What the Church teaches: Reason knows that God is; only revelation and faith open his inner life and friendship with him (§ 35).

To think about

Which "way" is most convincing to you: the world, or the human person? Why?

For catechists

Aim: Participants can present the two "starting points" (the world and the human person) for knowing God, and explain why they are reasonable.

  1. Why does anything exist rather than nothing?
  2. Where do your sense of right and wrong and your longing for meaning come from?
  3. Why does knowing that God exists differ from knowing him?

Activity: Go outside (or look at photos of nature). Each person chooses one thing and asks: what does this tell me about its Maker?
