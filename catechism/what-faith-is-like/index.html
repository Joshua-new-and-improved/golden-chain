What faith is like

What faith is like? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 153–165

In one sentence: Faith is a gift of grace and a truly human act: certain, seeking understanding, free, necessary for salvation, and a foretaste of heaven.

What the Catechism says

Faith is a gift: a supernatural virtue infused by God, with the interior help of the Spirit. Yet it is also an authentically human act, not contrary to freedom or reason. It is more certain than any human knowledge, because it rests on God who cannot lie, even if what it believes can seem obscure. Faith seeks understanding, and there can never be a real conflict between faith and reason, since the same God gives both.

Faith must be free: no one may be forced to believe. It is necessary for salvation. It can be lost, so we must nourish it with the word of God and beg the Lord to increase it. Faith is already the beginning of eternal life, even though we often walk in darkness and trial.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 153 When Peter confessed Jesus as the Christ, Jesus declared that this was revealed by the Father. Faith is a gift of God, a supernatural virtue infused by him: to believe, we need the grace of God and the interior help of the Holy Spirit.

§ 154–155 Believing is possible only by grace, yet it is also an authentically human act: trusting God and holding to his truths is not contrary to human freedom or reason. Even in human relations it is not contrary to our dignity to believe what others tell us. In faith the intellect and will cooperate with grace; St Thomas: believing is an act of the intellect assenting to divine truth by command of the will moved by God through grace.

§ 156 What moves us to believe is not that revealed truths appear true in the light of reason, but the authority of God himself, who can neither deceive nor be deceived. Yet so that our faith might be in accord with reason, God gave outward proofs of revelation: the miracles of Christ and the saints, prophecies, the Church's growth and holiness, her fruitfulness and stability. These are motives of credibility.

§ 157–159 Faith is certain, more certain than all human knowledge, because founded on God, though revealed truths can seem obscure to reason; "ten thousand difficulties do not make one doubt" (Newman). Faith seeks understanding: it is intrinsic to faith to want to know better the One in whom one has believed. Faith and reason cannot truly conflict, since the same God reveals mysteries and gives the light of reason; methodical research, if truly scientific and respectful of moral norms, never conflicts with faith.

§ 160–162 Faith must be free: no one is to be forced to embrace the faith against his will; Christ invited, he did not coerce. Faith is necessary for salvation ("without faith it is impossible to please God"). Faith is a free gift that can be lost: to persevere we must nourish it with the word of God, beg the Lord to increase it, and let it work through charity.

§ 163–165 Faith is the beginning of eternal life: it makes us taste in advance the joy of the beatific vision. Now, however, we walk by faith, not by sight; our experience of evil, suffering and death can shake it. Then we turn to the witnesses of faith: Abraham, who hoped against hope; Mary, who walked in the night of faith; and the "great cloud of witnesses".

Words to know

Theological virtue
A virtue given by God, directed to God: faith, hope and charity.
Fides quaerens intellectum
"Faith seeking understanding" (St Anselm).

In the Bible

  • Mark 9:23: I do believe; help my unbelief
  • Heb 11:1: faith is the substance of things hoped for

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST II-II q.2 a.9: The act of believing is meritorious because it comes from the will moved by grace.
  • ST II-II q.4 a.8: Faith is more certain than science or wisdom in its cause, God's truth, even if less clear to us.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “I do not seek to understand so that I may believe; I believe so that I may understand.” (St Anselm (Proslogion 1))
  • “Ten thousand difficulties do not make one doubt.” (St John Henry Newman (Apologia, 1864))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Having doubts means I have no faith.
    What the Church teaches: Difficulties are not the same as doubt; faith can be tested and grow (§§ 157, 164).
  • Not quite: Science disproves faith.
    What the Church teaches: Faith and reason cannot truly contradict each other; honest research never conflicts with faith (§ 159).
  • Not quite: Faith, once received, can never be lost.
    What the Church teaches: It is a gift that must be nourished, or it can be lost (§ 162).

To think about

Have you ever felt faith and reason pulling in different directions? What resolved it, or what would?

For catechists

Aim: Participants can describe the characteristics of faith and respond calmly to the claim that faith is irrational or opposed to science.

  1. What is the difference between a difficulty and a doubt (Newman)?
  2. Name scientists who were also people of faith (e.g. Georges Lemaître, a priest who proposed the Big Bang theory).
  3. How do you nourish your faith (§ 162)?

Activity: List "motives of credibility" (§ 156) you find convincing: a miracle, a saint's life, the Church's survival. Share one.
