What prayer is, and how God taught it

What prayer is, and how God taught it? · Catechism of the Catholic Church §§ 2558–2622

In one sentence: Prayer is the raising of the heart to God: a gift, a covenant and a communion, revealed from Abraham and the Psalms to Jesus and Mary.

What the Catechism says

"For me, prayer is a surge of the heart, a simple look turned toward heaven" (St Thérèse). Prayer is the raising of one's mind and heart to God. It is a gift (we pray from humility, as beggars), a covenant (it comes from the heart, the place of encounter), and a communion with the Trinity.

God calls every person to prayer. In the Old Testament it is revealed through Abraham, Moses, David, Elijah and the Psalms, the masterwork of prayer. In the fullness of time Jesus prays: he prays to his Father, teaches his disciples to pray with faith and perseverance, and hears prayer addressed to him. Mary's prayer, at the Annunciation and Cana, is a model.

Paragraph by paragraph

§ 2558–2565 "Great is the mystery of the faith!" The Church professes it in the Creed, celebrates it in the liturgy, lives it in the commandments, and prays it: "this mystery, then, requires that the faithful believe in it, that they celebrate it, and that they live from it in a vital and personal relationship with the living and true God". This relationship is prayer. "For me, prayer is a surge of the heart; it is a simple look turned toward heaven, it is a cry of recognition and of love, embracing both trial and joy" (Thérèse). Prayer is the raising of one's mind and heart to God, or the requesting of good things from God. Humility is its foundation: "we do not know how to pray as we ought." Prayer is a gift of God: "If you knew the gift of God!" The wonder of prayer is revealed beside the well where we come seeking water: there Christ comes to meet every human being; he seeks us first and asks us for a drink. "God thirsts that we may thirst for him" (Augustine). Prayer is covenant: it comes from the heart, the place of decision, of truth, of encounter. Prayer is communion: the life of prayer is the habit of being in the presence of the thrice-holy God and in communion with him.

§ 2566–2589 The universal call to prayer: man is in search of God. In the Old Testament, prayer is revealed through the covenant: Abraham (whose prayer is expressed first by deeds: he obeys, and then intercedes for Sodom); Jacob wrestling with the angel; Moses, the intercessor and friend of God ("face to face, as a man speaks to his friend"); David, the king "after God's own heart", and the Temple; Elijah and the prophets; and the Psalms, the masterwork of prayer in the Old Testament, which both nourish and express the prayer of the People of God gathered at the great feasts and the prayer of each person. They remain essential to the prayer of the Church.

§ 2598–2622 In the fullness of time: the drama of prayer is fully revealed in the Word made flesh. Jesus learned to pray with the words and rhythms of his people; he often prays alone, in solitude, especially before decisive moments (his baptism, the choice of the Twelve, the Transfiguration, his Passion). His prayer is a loving adherence of his human heart to the Father's will. His prayer before the raising of Lazarus begins with thanksgiving. At Gethsemane and on the Cross, his prayer takes up all the troubles of humanity. Jesus teaches us to pray: with a converted heart, with faith, with boldness, with watchfulness, persistence (the importunate friend, the unjust judge) and humility (the Pharisee and the tax collector). Jesus hears prayer: he answers prayer offered in faith. The prayer of the Virgin Mary: at the Annunciation (her fiat), at Cana (her intercession), at the foot of the Cross, and in the Magnificat, the song of the Mother of God and of the Church.

Words to know

Prayer
The raising of mind and heart to God, or the requesting of good things from him.

In the Bible

  • Ps 41:2-3 (42): my soul thirsteth for God
  • Luke 11:1-13: Lord, teach us to pray
  • Matt 26:39: not as I will, but as thou wilt

With St Thomas Aquinas

  • ST II-II q.83 a.1: Prayer is an act of reason, expressing our desire to God: "the interpreter of our desire".
  • ST II-II q.83 a.2: We pray not to change God's plan but to obtain what he has planned to give through our prayer.

From the Fathers and saints

  • “For me, prayer is a surge of the heart; it is a simple look turned toward heaven, a cry of recognition and of love, embracing both trial and joy.” (St Thérèse of Lisieux (Story of a Soul, Ms C 25r))
  • “Prayer is the raising of one's mind and heart to God or the requesting of good things from God.” (St John Damascene (On the Orthodox Faith III.24))
  • “God thirsts that we may thirst for him.” (St Augustine (Eighty-Three Questions 64.4))

Common misunderstandings

  • Not quite: Prayer is mainly asking God for things.
    What the Church teaches: Prayer is a living relationship with God: adoration, thanksgiving, contrition and petition, rooted in the heart (§§ 2558–2565).
  • Not quite: We have to persuade an unwilling God by praying.
    What the Church teaches: God seeks us first and thirsts for us; prayer is our response to his gift (§ 2560).
  • Not quite: The Psalms are Old Testament and no longer for Christians.
    What the Church teaches: They remain essential to the Church's prayer, and Jesus himself prayed them (§§ 2586–2587).

For catechists

Aim: Participants understand prayer as gift, covenant and communion, and learn from the great prayers of Scripture, Jesus and Mary.

  1. Which definition of prayer in § 2558–2559 speaks most to you?
  2. What do Abraham, Moses and Elijah teach about intercession?
  3. Why do you think Jesus prayed before every major decision?

Activity: Pray Psalm 23 or Psalm 51 slowly together. Then each person writes their own short prayer in the style of a psalm (praise, lament, trust).
